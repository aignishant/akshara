# 2.1 — Start big and prune

## One-line answer

Unigram runs the opposite way from BPE — it starts with **56,957** candidate substrings and throws away
98.2% of them to reach `V = 1000`, rather than starting at 256 and building up — and one measured
consequence is that training gets **faster** as `V` grows, where BPE gets slower.

---

## The story

Two people are packing for a trip with one small bag.

The first starts with an empty bag and adds things: socks, then a jumper, then a book, stopping when the
bag is full. Every decision is "is this worth adding?" and once something is in, it stays.

The second tips the whole wardrobe onto the bed, then removes whatever they would miss least, one item at a
time, until what is left fits. Every decision is "what would I miss least?" and every remaining item has
survived being questioned.

Both bags end up full. They do not contain the same things, and the second person had to look at everything
once.

---

## The idea in plain language

Every tokenizer you have built grows a vocabulary **upward**:

| Day | Starts with | Direction | Stops when |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 12 — BPE | 151 characters | add one merge at a time | `V` reached |
| 13 — byte BPE | 256 byte symbols | add one merge at a time | `V` reached |
| 15 — WordPiece | characters + `##` forms | add one merge at a time | `V` reached |

Unigram goes **downward**, and that single reversal changes almost everything about it:

1. **Build a large candidate pool** — every substring of every word, up to a length limit. Measured on this
   corpus: 56,957 distinct candidates.
2. **Fit a probability to each one** so the pool explains the corpus as well as possible. This is the EM
   loop, and part [2.2](2.2-every-token-carries-a-probability.md) is about the probabilities it produces.
3. **Score every candidate by how much the corpus would suffer without it.** Remove the least useful
   fraction.
4. **Repeat** until the pool is down to `V`.

Two consequences worth holding on to before the measurements.

**The vocabulary is not ordered.** There are no merges and no ranks — just a set of tokens, each with a
probability. Like WordPiece part [1.3](../01-wordpiece/1.3-greedy-longest-match-first.md), and unlike BPE,
you could shuffle the file. Unlike WordPiece, encoding is not greedy; part
[2.3](2.3-viterbi-picks-the-best-segmentation.md) is what it does instead.

**Every surviving token has been tested.** A BPE merge is added because it was frequent *at that moment*
and never reconsidered — Day 12 part
[2.3](../../../day-012-bpe-the-merge-loop/parts/02-training-a-vocabulary/2.3-reading-the-table.md) showed
merges from early in the list that later became useless and stayed anyway. In Unigram, a token that stops
earning its slot is removed on the next round. The final vocabulary is one nothing has yet argued against.

---

## Why Akshara needs it

**Because "add greedily" and "prune from a superset" are two shapes you will meet repeatedly.** Pruning a
trained network (Day 105, `EFF-*`) is this same move. So is feature selection, so is beam search. Meeting
it on a tokenizer, where you can print the whole state, is the cheapest possible introduction.

**Because it is the algorithm behind the tokenizers Akshara is most likely to use.** SentencePiece's
default model type is Unigram, and section 3 is where that connection is made concrete.

**Because it explains a genuinely counter-intuitive measurement.** Training gets *faster* as `V` grows.
When you can predict that from the direction of travel, you understand the algorithm rather than the API.

## The mechanism

### Step 1 — how big is the pool it starts from?

```python
import re
from collections import Counter

words = Counter(re.findall(r"\S+", text))
subs = Counter()
for w, n in words.items():
    for i in range(len(w)):
        for j in range(i + 1, min(i + 17, len(w)) + 1):
            subs[w[i:j]] += n

print(f"distinct substrings (<=16 chars): {len(subs):,}")
print(f"  seen at least twice           : {sum(1 for v in subs.values() if v >= 2):,}")
```

**Line by line:**

- `re.findall(r"\S+", text)` splits on whitespace only — a rough stand-in for the Metaspace pre-tokenizer
  of part [3.1](../03-sentencepiece/3.1-the-metaspace-trick.md). Candidates never cross a chunk boundary,
  the same rule Day 12 part
  [1.4](../../../day-012-bpe-the-merge-loop/parts/01-the-merge-loop/1.4-the-pre-tokenizer.md) established.
- The double loop enumerates **every substring** of every word: start at `i`, end at every `j` after it.
  This is `O(n²)` in word length, which is why the cap exists.
- `min(i + 17, len(w)) + 1` caps candidate length at 16 characters. Without a cap the pool would include
  every prefix of every long token in the corpus, most of which occur once. The library exposes this as
  `max_piece_length`.
- `+= n` weights by how often the containing word appeared, for the same reason as part
  [1.1](../01-wordpiece/1.1-scoring-a-pair-by-likelihood.md)'s counting.

Measured, corpus `9760f2b6d4340b97`:

```text
distinct substrings (<=16 chars): 56,957
  seen at least twice           : 28,432
```

**56,957 candidates for 1,000 slots.** The real trainer trims this further before it begins, but the shape
is right: Unigram considers roughly **57×** as many tokens as it will keep, where BPE considers exactly one
new token per slot and never revisits it.

That is the cost of the approach, and it buys the guarantee that nothing survived unexamined.

### Step 2 — train it

```python
from tokenizers import Tokenizer, models, pre_tokenizers, decoders, trainers

ug = Tokenizer(models.Unigram())
ug.pre_tokenizer = pre_tokenizers.Metaspace()
ug.decoder = decoders.Metaspace()
ug.train_from_iterator([text], trainers.UnigramTrainer(
    vocab_size=1000, show_progress=False,
    special_tokens=["<unk>"], unk_token="<unk>"))
print(ug.encode("The plan costs zero dollars.").tokens)
```

**Line by line:**

- `models.Unigram()` with no arguments — an empty model, filled by the trainer. Same pattern as
  `models.BPE()` on Day 14.
- `pre_tokenizers.Metaspace()` replaces each space with `▁` (U+2581) rather than discarding it. This is the
  fix for part [1.2](../01-wordpiece/1.2-the-hash-hash-convention.md)'s whitespace problem, and part
  [3.1](../03-sentencepiece/3.1-the-metaspace-trick.md) is where it gets its own treatment.
- `unk_token="<unk>"` **and** `special_tokens=["<unk>"]` — both. The first tells the model which token to
  emit for unknown input; the second puts it in the vocabulary. Setting only one is a configuration that
  looks complete and is not.
- No `initial_alphabet` argument here, unlike Day 14's BPE. Unigram's pool already contains every
  single character it saw, because every character is a length-1 substring.

Measured:

```text
['▁The', '▁plan', '▁cost', 's', '▁zero', '▁do', 'll', 'ar', 's', '.']
```

Ten tokens. Compare the same sentence under WordPiece from part 1.2:

```text
['The', 'plan', 'cost', '##s', 'zero', 'do', '##l', '##l', '##ar', '##s', '.']
```

Eleven. And note the difference in *shape*: Unigram writes `▁do`, `ll`, `ar`, `s` where WordPiece writes
`do`, `##l`, `##l`, `##ar`, `##s`. Unigram found `ll` as one piece; greedy matching could not, because at
that position it took the longest match `##l` and never looked further. Part
[2.3](2.3-viterbi-picks-the-best-segmentation.md) is why.

### Step 3 — the counter-intuitive timing

```python
for V in (300, 1000, 3000):
    t0 = time.perf_counter()
    # ... train at vocab_size=V ...
    print(f"V={V} train={time.perf_counter() - t0:.1f}s")
```

**Line by line:**

- The training call is elided because it is the same four lines as step 2 with `vocab_size=V`; the timing
  harness is what this block is about.
- `time.perf_counter()` around the **training** call only, not the encoding. The claim being tested is
  about how long it takes to *build* a vocabulary, and encoding afterwards would pollute it.
- Three sizes rather than two, because the interesting property is the *direction* of the trend and two
  points cannot show a trend that survives noise.

Measured on this machine:

| `V` | Unigram train | ids | chars/token |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 300 | **1.3 s** | 68,081 | 1.628 |
| 1,000 | **1.0 s** | 43,038 | 2.575 |
| 3,000 | **0.8 s** | 31,715 | 3.495 |

**Training gets faster as the vocabulary gets bigger.** That is backwards from everything Days 12–14
measured. Day 12 part
[2.2](../../../day-012-bpe-the-merge-loop/parts/02-training-a-vocabulary/2.2-training-to-a-target-size.md)
measured BPE at 5.8 s, 11.5 s, 25.9 s and 50.8 s for `V = 300, 500, 1000, 2000` — steadily slower, because
each additional slot is one more pass over the corpus.

The direction of travel explains both. BPE **adds** one token per slot, so more slots is more work.
Unigram **removes** a fraction of the pool per round, so a larger target means *fewer rounds* before it
stops. Same corpus, opposite slope, and you can predict the sign of it from one sentence of description.

(The comparison is across two implementations — Python for BPE, Rust for Unigram — so the absolute times
are not comparable. Only the slopes are, and it is the slopes that are the point.)

## Shapes

| Name | Symbolic | Concrete | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| candidate pool | `(n_candidates,)` | `(56957,)` | every substring up to 16 chars |
| pool seen twice or more | `(n_frequent,)` | `(28432,)` | the trainer's realistic starting set |
| final vocabulary | `(V,)` | `(1000,)` | after pruning; **98.2% discarded** |
| BPE's pool, for contrast | `(V - 256,)` | `(744,)` | exactly one candidate per slot |

`1 - 1000/56957 = 98.2%`. Almost everything Unigram considers, it rejects.

---

## When it breaks

**The candidate pool is quadratic in word length and it will surprise you.** No `max_piece_length` cap and
one 10,000-character "word" — a base64 blob, a minified line — contributes about 50 million candidates on
its own. The symptom is a trainer that appears to hang. This is the same input that turned into `[UNK]`
under WordPiece in part [1.3](../01-wordpiece/1.3-greedy-longest-match-first.md); the two algorithms fail
on it in opposite directions, one silently and one loudly.

**A vocabulary smaller than the alphabet is impossible, and the failure is confusing.** Ask for `V = 50` on
a corpus with 151 distinct characters and the trainer cannot prune below the characters it must keep to
represent anything. What you get is not an error but a vocabulary that ignores your request.

**`shrinking_factor` changes the vocabulary, not just the speed.** It controls what fraction is removed per
round — the default removes 25% at a time. A different value visits different intermediate states, and
because each round's probabilities are fitted to the *current* pool, the final vocabulary differs. Like
Day 14's tie-break, it is a setting that lives in the trainer, is not recorded in the output, and changes
the ids.

**Comparing training times across implementations proves nothing.** The table above compares Rust Unigram
against Python BPE. The 1.3 s and the 5.8 s are not commensurable, and only the *slopes* — one rising, one
falling — carry the argument. Stating that explicitly is the difference between a measurement and a
misleading measurement.

---

## In production

**Pruning-from-a-superset is the more expensive half of a real trade.** Unigram must hold a large candidate
pool and repeatedly re-fit probabilities over it, so its memory footprint at corpus scale is far above
BPE's. In exchange, every surviving token has been evaluated in the context of the final vocabulary rather
than an early one.

**This is why SentencePiece defaults to Unigram**, and part [3.1](../03-sentencepiece/3.1-the-metaspace-trick.md)
picks that up. The pruning approach pairs naturally with the probabilistic encoding of part 2.3 and the
sampling of part [2.4](2.4-subword-regularization.md) — none of which BPE can offer, because a merge list
has no probabilities in it to sample from.

**The measured compression difference is small.** Part
[4.1](../04-together/4.1-nine-tokenizers-one-table.md) puts Unigram, WordPiece and byte-level BPE side by
side at `V = 1000` on this corpus, and they land within a few percent. Choose between these families on
the properties that differ sharply — round-trip safety, unknown handling, sampling — not on compression,
where the honest answer is that it barely matters.

**The EM loop is the same machinery as everywhere else.** Fit parameters, evaluate, prune, refit. You will
run the same shape on Day 105 when pruning a network and on Day 118 when selecting eval subsets. The
tokenizer version is the one where you can print the entire state and read it.

## Check yourself

```python
import re, time
from collections import Counter
from pathlib import Path
from tokenizers import Tokenizer, models, pre_tokenizers, decoders, trainers

text = Path("docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md").read_text(encoding="utf-8")

words = Counter(re.findall(r"\S+", text))
subs = Counter()
for w, n in words.items():
    for i in range(len(w)):
        for j in range(i + 1, min(i + 17, len(w)) + 1):
            subs[w[i:j]] += n
print(f"candidate pool: {len(subs):,} -> keeping 1,000 = {1000/len(subs)*100:.1f}%")
assert len(subs) > 10 * 1000, "your pool is not much bigger than V - the pruning story is invisible"

def build(V):
    t = Tokenizer(models.Unigram())
    t.pre_tokenizer = pre_tokenizers.Metaspace()
    t.decoder = decoders.Metaspace()
    t.train_from_iterator([text], trainers.UnigramTrainer(
        vocab_size=V, show_progress=False, special_tokens=["<unk>"], unk_token="<unk>"))
    return t

# the eval that can go red: training time must FALL as V rises
times, ratios = [], []
for V in (300, 1000, 3000):
    t0 = time.perf_counter(); tok = build(V); times.append(time.perf_counter() - t0)
    n = len(tok.encode(text).ids); ratios.append(len(text) / n)
    print(f"V={V:5d} train={times[-1]:5.2f}s chars/tok={ratios[-1]:.3f}")

assert ratios == sorted(ratios), "compression must improve with V"
assert times == sorted(times, reverse=True), \
    "training did not get faster with a larger V - re-run; on a loaded machine timings are noisy"

# unknown handling, and a trap: check IDS, never token strings
tok = build(1000)
UNK = tok.token_to_id("<unk>")
for p in ["café", "naïve", "résumé"]:
    out = tok.encode(p)
    n_unk = sum(1 for i in out.ids if i == UNK)
    print(f"  {p!r:10s} tokens={out.tokens} ids={out.ids} unknown={n_unk}/{len(out.ids)}")
    # the trap, asserted so you cannot walk past it:
    assert "<unk>" not in out.tokens, "the token STRING never says <unk> - see below"
    assert n_unk > 0, f"{p!r} was fully covered - your corpus contains é, so pick another probe"
    # and the property that matters: only the missing CHARACTER is lost, not the word
    assert len(out.ids) - n_unk >= 2, "the rest of the word survived, unlike WordPiece"
```

**Line by line:**

- `assert times == sorted(times, reverse=True)` is the eval that can go red, and it asserts the
  counter-intuitive claim rather than the comfortable one. Its message admits the honest weakness: timings
  are noisy, and a loaded machine can invert a 0.2 s gap. A test whose failure message tells you when to
  disbelieve it is more useful than one that does not.
- `assert len(subs) > 10 * 1000` guards the precondition, the same reflex as part 1.1's probe guard. If
  your corpus is tiny the pool is not much larger than `V` and the whole lesson is invisible rather than
  wrong.
- **The two assertions in the last block look contradictory and both pass**, which is the point. Measured
  on `café`:

  ```text
  tokens=['▁ca', 'f', 'é']   ids=[339, 40, 0]   unknown=1/3
  ```

  The token string reads `'é'`. The id is `0`, and `tok.id_to_token(0)` is `'<unk>'`. **`.tokens` reports
  the surface text that was consumed, not the vocabulary entry that consumed it.** So
  `"<unk>" not in out.tokens` passes while the token *is* unknown — a test that looks correct, runs
  clean, and checks nothing.

  This is Day 14 part
  [3.3](../../../day-014-tiktoken-and-the-pipeline/parts/03-the-remaining-gap/3.3-the-mismatch-the-library-will-not-catch.md)'s
  rule arriving from a direction that day never showed you: **compare ids, not strings** — not only across
  two tokenizers, but within one.
- `len(out.ids) - n_unk >= 2` is the property that distinguishes this family from WordPiece. `café` loses
  one *character* here and its whole *word* in part
  [1.4](../01-wordpiece/1.4-the-word-that-became-one-unk.md). Both are losses; they are not the same size.
  Part [3.2](../03-sentencepiece/3.2-byte-fallback.md) removes even this one.
