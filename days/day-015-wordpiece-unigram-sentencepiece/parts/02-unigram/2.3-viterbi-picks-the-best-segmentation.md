# 2.3 — Viterbi picks the best segmentation

## One-line answer

`▁dollars` has **8** valid segmentations under this vocabulary and Unigram returns the highest-scoring one
— `['▁do', 'll', 'ar', 's']` at `-23.4779` against the worst at `-35.6886`, making it **201,000× more
likely** — found by dynamic programming in twenty lines that agree with the library on **6 of 6** probes.

---

## The story

You are driving to a town and there are several routes. Each road has a time on the map.

The greedy way is to take the fastest road out of every junction as you reach it. It is easy, you never
have to plan, and it is not the quickest route — a fast road early can dump you onto a slow one later.

The proper way is to work out, for every junction, the best possible time to reach *it* from the start.
Then the best time to reach the town is the best time to reach some junction plus the road from there. You
compute each junction once, in order, and the answer falls out — and you have not tried every route,
because most routes share their beginnings.

---

## The idea in plain language

Part [1.3](../01-wordpiece/1.3-greedy-longest-match-first.md) showed WordPiece encoding greedily: take the
longest match, move on, never reconsider. Fast, and demonstrably not optimal — it produced
`do`+`##l`+`##l`+`##ar`+`##s` where a better split existed.

Unigram has something greedy matching does not: **a score for every possible segmentation**. Part
[2.2](2.2-every-token-carries-a-probability.md) established that the score of a segmentation is the sum of
its pieces' log probabilities. So encoding becomes a search:

```text
of all the ways to cut this word into vocabulary pieces,
return the one whose total log probability is highest
```

Enumerating them is hopeless in general — a word of `n` characters has up to `2^(n-1)` segmentations. But
you never need to enumerate, because of one observation:

> **The best segmentation of the first `i` characters ends with exactly one piece.** If you already know
> the best score for every position before `i`, then the best score at `i` is the best over each possible
> last piece, of (best score where that piece starts) + (the piece's own score).

That turns an exponential search into a loop over positions, each doing a loop over possible last pieces —
`O(n²)` with a length cap, and `O(n)` positions. This is **dynamic programming**, and in this setting it is
called the **Viterbi algorithm**.

The result is a genuine optimum, not a heuristic. Where WordPiece returns *a* segmentation, Unigram returns
*the best* segmentation under its model — and can tell you the score of the one it rejected.

---

## Why Akshara needs it

**Because dynamic programming over a sequence is the single most reused algorithm shape in the
curriculum.** Beam search on Day 45 is this with a width limit. The KV cache on Day 42 is this insight
applied to attention — reuse the prefix computation instead of redoing it. Meeting it on a seven-character
word, where you can print the whole table, is the cheapest possible first encounter.

**Because it is the payoff for part 2.2's probabilities.** Numbers in a vocabulary are only interesting if
something uses them to choose. This is the something.

**Because you can prove your implementation is right.** Twenty lines against a Rust library on real input
is Principle 3's whole argument, and this is the second time today it has come out clean.

## The mechanism

### Step 1 — how many segmentations are there really?

```python
def all_segmentations(w, vocab):
    """Every way to cut w into pieces that are all in vocab."""
    out = []
    def rec(i, acc):
        if i == len(w):
            out.append(list(acc))
            return
        for j in range(i + 1, len(w) + 1):
            if w[i:j] in vocab:
                acc.append(w[i:j])
                rec(j, acc)
                acc.pop()
    rec(0, [])
    return out
```

**Line by line:**

- This is the brute-force enumeration, written **only** to check the fast version. It is exactly the role
  Day 13 part
  [1.1](../../../day-013-bpe-encode-decode-bytes/parts/01-encode-and-decode/1.1-encoding-applies-merges-by-rank.md)
  gave the slow sequential encoder: a test oracle, never the shipped path.
- `rec(j, acc)` recurses from where the piece ended. `acc.pop()` undoes the choice on the way back out —
  standard backtracking, and the reason `acc` can be reused instead of copied at every level.
- `list(acc)` when recording, because `acc` is about to be mutated. Appending `acc` itself would give you
  `len(out)` references to one list that ends up empty.
- Exponential in the worst case. Safe here only because the word is short.

Measured on `▁dollars`, corpus `9760f2b6d4340b97`:

```text
8 valid segmentations
```

Only eight, not `2⁷ = 128`, because most cuts produce pieces that are not in the vocabulary. The
vocabulary prunes the search enormously — and that is not something you can rely on for a longer word.

### Step 2 — score them all

```python
scored = sorted(((sum(V[p] for p in seg), seg) for seg in segs), reverse=True)
for total, seg in scored[:6]:
    print(f"  {total:9.4f}  {seg}")
```

**Line by line:**

- `sum(V[p] for p in seg)` — the score of a segmentation is the **sum of logs**, which is part 2.2's
  product of probabilities in log space. Adding, not multiplying.
- `sorted(..., reverse=True)` on `(score, seg)` tuples puts the highest score first. Scores are negative,
  so "highest" means "closest to zero".

Measured:

```text
   -23.4779  ['▁do', 'll', 'ar', 's']
   -27.0799  ['▁do', 'll', 'a', 'r', 's']
   -27.2734  ['▁do', 'l', 'l', 'ar', 's']
   -28.2910  ['▁', 'd', 'o', 'll', 'ar', 's']
   -30.8754  ['▁do', 'l', 'l', 'a', 'r', 's']
   -31.8931  ['▁', 'd', 'o', 'll', 'a', 'r', 's']
   ...
   -32.0865  ['▁', 'd', 'o', 'l', 'l', 'ar', 's']
   -35.6886  ['▁', 'd', 'o', 'l', 'l', 'a', 'r', 's']
```

And what the tokenizer actually returned:

```text
['▁do', 'll', 'ar', 's']    <- the argmax
```

**The gap is enormous.** Best `-23.4779`, worst `-35.6886`, a difference of `12.21` in log space:

```python
math.exp(-23.4779 - (-35.6886))   # 2.01e+05
```

**Line by line:**

- Subtracting two log probabilities and exponentiating gives their **ratio**, not their difference. That is
  the arithmetic that turns "12.21 apart" into "201,000 times more likely" — and the reason a gap of 12 in
  log space is enormous rather than modest.
- The numbers are pasted from the measured output above rather than recomputed, so this line is a
  conversion you can check by eye.

The chosen segmentation is **201,000 times more likely** than the all-characters one under this model. That
is why the sum-of-logs is worth computing: the candidates are not close.

Notice also the ordering is sensible — the more the segmentation falls back to single characters, the worse
it scores, because each additional piece multiplies in another small probability. **The model prefers
longer pieces automatically**, without any rule saying so. Compare WordPiece, where "longest first" had to
be written into the algorithm by hand.

### Step 3 — the algorithm that finds it without enumerating

```python
def viterbi(word: str, logp: dict[str, float]) -> tuple[list[str], float]:
    """Best segmentation of `word` under a unigram model. Returns (pieces, total log-prob)."""
    n = len(word)
    best = [-math.inf] * (n + 1)                # best[i] = best log-prob of word[:i]
    back = [(-1, "")] * (n + 1)
    best[0] = 0.0
    for i in range(1, n + 1):
        for j in range(i):                      # word[j:i] is the last piece
            piece = word[j:i]
            s = logp.get(piece)
            if s is not None and best[j] > -math.inf and best[j] + s > best[i]:
                best[i] = best[j] + s
                back[i] = (j, piece)
    if best[n] == -math.inf:
        return [], -math.inf
    pieces, i = [], n
    while i > 0:
        j, piece = back[i]
        pieces.append(piece)
        i = j
    return pieces[::-1], best[n]
```

**Line by line:**

- `best[i]` is "the best total log probability of any segmentation of the **first `i` characters**". The
  array is `n + 1` long because position 0 (the empty prefix) is a real state.
- `best[0] = 0.0` — the empty prefix costs nothing, because `log 1 = 0`. That is the identity for a sum of
  logs, exactly as `1` is for a product, and getting it wrong (say `-inf`) makes every path unreachable.
- The outer loop goes **forward** over end positions, so when `i` is reached every `best[j]` for `j < i` is
  already final. That ordering is what makes one pass sufficient.
- `logp.get(piece)` returns `None` for a piece not in the vocabulary, which the guard skips. Using
  `logp.get(piece, -inf)` instead would also work but would do arithmetic on infinities for no reason.
- `s is not None and best[j] > -math.inf` — the second half is essential and easy to omit. If position `j`
  is unreachable, extending from it is meaningless; without the check you would compute `-inf + s`, which
  is `-inf`, and *usually* lose the comparison — but it makes the intent unclear and it interacts badly
  with `back` bookkeeping.
- `back[i] = (j, piece)` records **where the best path into `i` came from**. This is the whole
  reconstruction mechanism: the forward pass computes scores, the backward walk recovers the choices.
- `if best[n] == -math.inf: return []` — the honest failure. Nothing can segment this word. In a real
  tokenizer this is where `<unk>` or byte fallback takes over; part
  [3.2](../03-sentencepiece/3.2-byte-fallback.md) is the good answer.
- `pieces[::-1]` because the backward walk collects them last-to-first.

### Step 4 — check it against the library

```python
for p in ["dollars", "costs", "tokenizer", "plan", "vocabulary", "budget"]:
    mine, score = viterbi("▁" + p, LOGP)
    theirs = ug.encode(p).tokens
```

**Line by line:**

- `"▁" + p` prepends the metaspace marker by hand, because the library's pre-tokenizer does it before the

- `LOGP` excludes `<unk>`, whose sentinel score of `0.0` would otherwise beat every real token — part 2.2's

## Shapes

| Name | Symbolic | `▁dollars` | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `word` | `(n,)` | `(8,)` | characters, including `▁` |
| `best` | `(n + 1,)` | `(9,)` | best log-prob of each prefix; `best[0] = 0` |
| `back` | `(n + 1, 2)` | `(9, 2)` | `(start, piece)` of the winning last step |
| valid segmentations | scalar | `8` | what brute force enumerates |
| Viterbi work | `O(n²)` | `≈ 36` | prefix/piece pairs examined |

`best` and `back` are parallel arrays indexed by **position**, not by piece — the standard shape for
sequence DP, and the one you will write again for beam search.

---

## When it breaks

**Forgetting `best[0] = 0.0`.** Leave it at `-inf` and every position is unreachable, so the function
returns `[]` for every input. Loud, at least — but the symptom (empty output for everything) looks like a
vocabulary problem rather than an initialization one.

**Multiplying instead of adding.** Part 2.2's underflow arrives here concretely: with probabilities
instead of logs, a long word's segmentations all evaluate to `0.0` and the argmax becomes whichever the
comparison happened to reach first. The output is a valid segmentation, so nothing looks wrong.

**Leaving `<unk>` in the score table is harmless here, and that is worth knowing precisely.** Its score is
`0.0` — the maximum possible — so the obvious worry is that every word collapses to a single `<unk>`.
Measured, it does not:

```text
with <unk> in LOGP:  viterbi("▁dollars") -> (['▁do', 'll', 'ar', 's'], -23.4779)   <- unchanged
```

The reason is structural: `viterbi` only ever looks up `word[j:i]`, a **substring of the input**. The
literal seven-character string `<unk>` is not a substring of `▁dollars`, so it is never a candidate no
matter what it scores. The trap is real in part [2.2](2.2-every-token-carries-a-probability.md), where
summing over vocabulary entries *does* touch it and inflates the mass to 1.97. Two functions, one table,
and the sentinel matters in exactly one of them.

**Unknown characters take a different path entirely.** When a character has no token at all — `é` on this
corpus — the piece cannot be scored and Viterbi cannot cover that position. The library resolves it by
emitting `<unk>`'s id with the surface text attached, which part 2.1's check-yourself measured:
`tokens=['▁ca', 'f', 'é']` with `ids=[339, 40, 0]`. The token string says `é`; the id says unknown.

**Not capping piece length.** The inner loop `for j in range(i)` considers every possible start, so a
10,000-character word does 50 million dictionary lookups. Real implementations bound the piece length,
which also bounds the inner loop — the same cap that bounded part 2.1's candidate pool.

**Ties in the tail resolve arbitrarily.** Part 2.2 measured 14 tokens sharing the floor score
`-11.2305`. Two segmentations of a rare word can therefore score identically, and which one wins depends
on the iteration order — `>` versus `>=` in the comparison flips it. This is Day 14 part
[2.2](../../../day-014-tiktoken-and-the-pipeline/parts/02-the-tokenizers-pipeline/2.2-the-same-vocabulary-trained-twice.md)'s
tie-break problem in a new location, and it has the same answer: pick a rule, write it down, and expect
another implementation to have picked differently.

## In production

**This is the same algorithm as beam search, with the width set to one.** Day 45 (`INFER-*`) generalizes
it: keep the best `k` paths per position instead of the best one. If you understand `best[]` and `back[]`
here, that day is a small step rather than a new idea.

**Optimality is relative to a model that is known to be wrong.** Viterbi returns the exact argmax under the
unigram assumption — that tokens are independent. It is genuinely optimal for that model, and the model
does not describe language. "Optimal segmentation" always means "optimal under the scoring function you
chose", and being precise about that distinction is most of what makes a claim honest.

**Encoding is slower than BPE's and it does not matter.** Viterbi is `O(n²)` per word against BPE's near
linear rank lookups, but words are short and the cap makes the constant small. Day 14 part
[1.1](../../../day-014-tiktoken-and-the-pipeline/parts/01-tiktoken/1.1-the-same-corpus-through-both.md)'s
conclusion applies unchanged: tokenization is a one-off preprocessing pass, and the ranking of these costs
does not decide anything.

**The scores are a debugging tool nothing else in this section offers.** When a word segments oddly, you
can print every alternative with its score and see *by how much* the choice was made. A near-tie means the
vocabulary is ambivalent; a gap of 12 in log space means it is certain. BPE can tell you what it did and
never why.

## Check yourself

```python
import json, math
from pathlib import Path

V = {t: s for t, s in json.loads(ug.to_str())["model"]["vocab"] if t != "<unk>"}

# 1. the eval that can go red: your DP against brute force
for probe in ["dollars", "costs", "budget", "plan"]:
    w = "▁" + probe
    segs = all_segmentations(w, V)
    brute = max(segs, key=lambda s: sum(V[p] for p in s))
    mine, score = viterbi(w, V)
    assert mine == brute, f"{probe!r}: DP {mine} != brute force {brute}"
    assert abs(score - sum(V[p] for p in brute)) < 1e-9, "scores disagree"
    print(f"  {probe!r:10s} {len(segs):3d} segmentations, DP == brute force")

# 2. and against the library
for probe in ["dollars", "costs", "tokenizer", "plan", "vocabulary", "budget"]:
    assert viterbi("▁" + probe, V)[0] == ug.encode(probe).tokens, probe
print("6/6 agree with the library")

# 3. the gap is large - the candidates are not close
segs = all_segmentations("▁dollars", V)
scores = sorted(sum(V[p] for p in s) for s in segs)
print(f"best {scores[-1]:.4f}  worst {scores[0]:.4f}  "
      f"ratio {math.exp(scores[-1] - scores[0]):.3g}x")
assert scores[-1] - scores[0] > 5, "the segmentations scored close together - inspect the vocabulary"

# 4. <unk> scores 0.0 - the maximum - yet it cannot win. Show why.
with_unk = dict(V); with_unk["<unk>"] = 0.0
same, score = viterbi("▁dollars", with_unk)
print(f"with <unk> in the table: {same} scoring {score:.4f}")
assert same == viterbi("▁dollars", V)[0], \
    "adding <unk> changed the answer - it should be unreachable, being no substring of the word"
assert "<unk>" not in "▁dollars", "the sentinel is not a substring, which is the whole reason"
```

**Line by line:**

- Check 1 is the eval that can go red, and it is the strongest kind: the fast algorithm against an
  independent slow one, on the same input. Not "does it look right" but "does it equal the exhaustive
  answer". Both the segmentation *and* the score are compared, because a DP can find the right pieces with
  a wrong total if the recurrence is subtly off.
- `abs(score - ...) < 1e-9` rather than `==` on floats, because the DP adds in a different order from the
  brute-force sum and floating-point addition is not associative.
- Check 4 asserts a **non**-effect, which is the more useful thing here. `<unk>` scores `0.0`, the highest
  score in the table, and it still cannot win — because Viterbi only proposes substrings of the input.
  Pinning that down stops you "fixing" a bug that does not exist, and it makes the contrast with part 2.2
  sharp: the same sentinel in the same table is inert in one function and wrong by 1.0 in the other.
