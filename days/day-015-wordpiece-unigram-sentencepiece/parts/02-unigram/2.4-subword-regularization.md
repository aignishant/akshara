# 2.4 — Subword regularization

## One-line answer

Because every segmentation has a probability, you can **sample** one instead of taking the argmax — measured
here, 400 encodings of `dollars` produce **8 distinct segmentations**, and the `alpha` knob moves the
argmax's share from **86/400** to **256/400**, deliberately trading determinism for training-time variety.

---

## The story

A child learning to read sees the word *butterfly* only ever printed one way, always broken across the same
two lines: *butter-* then *-fly*. They learn it perfectly. Then they meet it in the middle of a line, whole,
and hesitate — they had learned the two halves and the break, not the word.

A different child meets it split in a dozen places over a year, and whole, and hyphenated. Slower at first.
Much steadier afterwards, because they never had the chance to memorise one particular shape.

Seeing the same thing described several ways is not noise. It is what stops you learning the description
instead of the thing.

---

## The idea in plain language

Part [2.3](2.3-viterbi-picks-the-best-segmentation.md) established that Unigram can score every
segmentation. So far it has used that to return the single best one, every time — deterministically.

But the scores are probabilities, and a distribution can be **sampled**. That is subword regularization,
introduced in arXiv:1804.10959 *"Subword Regularization: Improving Neural Network Translation Models with
Multiple Subword Candidates"*, and the idea is:

> During **training**, encode each occurrence of a word with a *randomly chosen* good segmentation rather
> than always the best one. During **inference**, go back to the argmax.

Why that helps is the child and the word. A model that only ever sees `dollars` as `▁do|ll|ar|s` learns
that particular sequence of four ids. Show it `▁do|l|l|ar|s` and `▁|d|o|ll|ar|s` too, and it has to learn
something about the *word* rather than about one arbitrary carving of it. The tokenizer's segmentation
choice — which part [2.3](2.3-viterbi-picks-the-best-segmentation.md) showed is a decision, not a fact —
stops being something the model can overfit to.

Two controls:

- **`nbest_size`** — how many of the top segmentations are eligible. Larger means more variety.
- **`alpha`** — a temperature. The sampling probability of a segmentation is proportional to
  `P(segmentation)^alpha`. Small `alpha` flattens the distribution toward uniform; large `alpha` sharpens
  it toward the argmax.

**This is only possible because the vocabulary carries numbers.** BPE and WordPiece have no distribution to
sample from — there is one merge order and one greedy walk. It is the clearest thing Unigram can do that
the other two structurally cannot.

---

## Why Akshara needs it

**Because it is a regularizer, and regularizers are a category you will use repeatedly.** Dropout on Day
34, weight decay on Day 25, data augmentation throughout — all the same move: add controlled variation at
training time so the model cannot memorise an accident of the data. This is that move applied to the
tokenizer, which is a place most people never think to look.

**Because it breaks an assumption Day 14 spent a whole day building.** Every check in Day 14 part
[3.3](../../../day-014-tiktoken-and-the-pipeline/parts/03-the-remaining-gap/3.3-the-mismatch-the-library-will-not-catch.md)
assumed a tokenizer is a deterministic function. Turn this on and it is not. Part
[2.5](2.5-the-tokenizer-that-disagreed-with-itself.md) is that collision, and it is why this part comes
first.

**Because the train/inference asymmetry is a trap with a name.** Sampling during training and forgetting to
turn it off at inference is a specific, silent, plausible bug — and it is Silent Failure #2 wearing yet
another costume.

---

## The mechanism

### Step 1 — the default is deterministic

```python
m = ug.model
print("nbest_size =", m.nbest_size, " alpha =", m.alpha)
print([ug.encode("dollars").tokens for _ in range(3)])
```

**Line by line:**

- `ug.model` reaches the stage-3 object from Day 14 part
  [2.1](../../../day-014-tiktoken-and-the-pipeline/parts/02-the-tokenizers-pipeline/2.1-five-stages-not-one.md)'s
  five. Sampling is a property of the *model*, not of the tokenizer as a whole, which is why it survives
  saving and loading — and why it is easy to forget it is set.
- Encoding the same string three times in one line makes non-determinism impossible to miss when it
  appears.

Measured, `tokenizers==0.23.1`:

```text
nbest_size = None  alpha = None
[['▁do', 'll', 'ar', 's'], ['▁do', 'll', 'ar', 's'], ['▁do', 'll', 'ar', 's']]
```

`None` and `None` mean "no sampling" — pure Viterbi, the argmax from part 2.3, three identical results.
**Sampling is opt-in**, which is the correct default and the reason you can use a Unigram tokenizer for
years without meeting any of this.

### Step 2 — turn it on

```python
m.nbest_size = 8
m.alpha = 0.1
counts = Counter(tuple(ug.encode("dollars").tokens) for _ in range(400))
print(f"{len(counts)} distinct segmentations in 400 draws")
for seg, n in counts.most_common(5):
    print(f"   {n:4d}/400 {list(seg)}")
```

**Line by line:**

- `m.nbest_size = 8` — consider the top 8 segmentations. Part 2.3 measured that `▁dollars` has exactly 8
  valid segmentations, so this makes all of them eligible. On a longer word it would be a genuine cut.
- `m.alpha = 0.1` is the flattening temperature. Below 1 it pushes the distribution toward uniform.
- `tuple(...)` because a `Counter` key must be hashable and `.tokens` is a list.
- 400 draws is enough to see the shape and small enough to run instantly. It is a sample, so your counts
  will differ from mine — the *shape* is the finding, not the numbers.

Measured, `alpha = 0.1`:

```text
8 distinct segmentations in 400 draws
     86/400 ['▁do', 'll', 'ar', 's']
     73/400 ['▁do', 'l', 'l', 'ar', 's']
     51/400 ['▁do', 'll', 'a', 'r', 's']
     43/400 ['▁', 'd', 'o', 'll', 'ar', 's']
     41/400 ['▁', 'd', 'o', 'll', 'a', 'r', 's']
```

**All 8 segmentations appear.** The argmax gets 86 of 400 — 21.5%, barely more than uniform's 12.5%. Part
2.3 measured that the best segmentation is 201,000× more likely than the worst under the raw model; at
`alpha = 0.1` that overwhelming preference has been almost entirely flattened away.

### Step 3 — `alpha` is a temperature

```python
m.alpha = 0.5
counts = Counter(tuple(ug.encode("dollars").tokens) for _ in range(400))
```

**Line by line:**

- Only `alpha` changes; `nbest_size` stays at 8 from the previous block. One variable at a time, which is
  the discipline Day 14 part
  [1.1](../../../day-014-tiktoken-and-the-pipeline/parts/01-tiktoken/1.1-the-same-corpus-through-both.md)
  built its whole argument on.
- The same 400 draws, so the two `Counter`s are directly comparable.

Measured, `alpha = 0.5`:

```text
7 distinct segmentations in 400 draws
    256/400 ['▁do', 'll', 'ar', 's']
     50/400 ['▁do', 'l', 'l', 'ar', 's']
     47/400 ['▁do', 'll', 'a', 'r', 's']
     32/400 ['▁', 'd', 'o', 'll', 'ar', 's']
      5/400 ['▁', 'd', 'o', 'll', 'a', 'r', 's']
```

Side by side, the knob's whole behaviour — two independent runs of 400 draws each, because **these are
samples and yours will differ**:

| | `alpha = 0.1` | `alpha = 0.5` | `alpha = None` |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| distinct segmentations, run 1 | 8 | 7 | 1 |
| distinct segmentations, run 2 | 8 | 8 | 1 |
| argmax share, run 1 | 86/400 = **21.5%** | 256/400 = **64.0%** | 400/400 = **100%** |
| argmax share, run 2 | 106/400 = **26.5%** | 265/400 = **66.3%** | 400/400 = **100%** |

The argmax share moves by five points between runs and the *ordering* never does: `alpha = 0.5`
concentrates more than `alpha = 0.1`, both times. That is the finding. Quoting `21.5%` as though it were a
property of the tokenizer would be quoting a sample as a constant — and with 400 draws the sampling error
is around two points, which is the arithmetic that decides how many draws you need.

**Raising `alpha` concentrates the distribution on the argmax.** At 0.1 the tokenizer is nearly uniform
over good segmentations; at 0.5 it usually agrees with Viterbi; with sampling off it always does. One
number moves you continuously between "maximum variety" and "deterministic".

This is the same shape as the sampling temperature you will meet on Day 44 (`INFER-*`) when choosing the
model's next token — flatten the distribution for variety, sharpen it for reliability. Meeting the knob
here, where you can enumerate the whole outcome space, makes that day cheaper.

### Step 4 — every sample still round-trips

```python
probes = ["plan", "The plan costs zero.", "zero budget", "Akshara"]
print(all(ug.decode(ug.encode(p).ids) == p for p in probes for _ in range(20)))
```

**Line by line:**

- The nested comprehension runs every probe twenty times — eighty encodings — because a single encoding of
  each would likely return the argmax and prove nothing about the sampled ones.
- `all(...)` collapses it to one boolean, which is the honest summary: either every sampled encoding is
  valid or the feature is broken. There is no interesting middle.

```text
True
```

**Eighty encodings, eighty exact round trips.** Every segmentation is a *valid* segmentation — they differ
in where the cuts fall, not in what they represent. Sampling changes the ids and not the text.

Which is precisely why it is dangerous, and precisely why the round-trip test — now failing to catch a real
problem for the fifth day running — will not warn you. Part 2.5 is that story.

## Shapes

| Name | Symbolic | Concrete | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `ids`, sampling off | `(T,)` | `(4,)` | always the same 4 |
| `ids`, `alpha=0.1` | `(T,)` | `(4,)` … `(8,)` | **`T` itself varies** |
| distinct outputs in 400 draws | scalar | `8` | the whole eligible set |
| corpus tokens, sampling off | `(T,)` | `(43038,)` | deterministic |
| corpus tokens, `alpha=0.1` | `(T,)` | `(60900,)`, `(60978,)`, `(61201,)` | three runs, three lengths |

The last row is the one to carry into part 2.5. **The sequence length of your training data is no longer a
fixed number** — and it is 42% larger, because flattened sampling favours shorter, more numerous pieces.
Every batch-size and context-window calculation that assumed a fixed token count is now approximate.

---

## When it breaks

**Leaving sampling on at inference.** The most common real bug of this feature. Training wanted variety;
serving wants the argmax. Because `nbest_size` and `alpha` live on the model object and travel in the saved
file, a tokenizer saved mid-training carries them, and the serving stack inherits sampling without anyone
choosing it. The symptom is a model that gives slightly different answers to the identical prompt, which
gets blamed on the sampler in the decoding loop.

**The token count is no longer fixed**, as the Shapes table measures: 60,900 / 60,978 / 61,201 on three
runs of the same corpus. Anything that pre-computes a number of batches, allocates a fixed buffer, or logs
"tokens seen" becomes approximate.

**More tokens means more compute for the same text.** 43,038 deterministic against about 61,000 sampled is
**42% more tokens** for the same corpus, and attention is `O(T²)`. Subword regularization is not free; it
buys robustness with training time, and the exchange rate is worth measuring before you accept it.

**A test suite that encodes and compares becomes flaky.** Not wrong — flaky, which the plan calls
indistinguishable from Silent Failure #4 and requires fixing rather than re-running. Any test touching a
sampling tokenizer must either seed it, disable sampling, or assert a property rather than an exact value.

---

## In production

**Sampling belongs in the training data pipeline, not in the tokenizer you ship.** The clean arrangement is
to sample when generating training batches and to save the tokenizer with sampling **off**, so the artifact
that travels is deterministic. Anything else relies on every consumer knowing to turn it off.

**The reported benefit is largest where data is scarce.** arXiv:1804.10959 §5 reports BLEU improvements
on machine translation, with the largest gains on low-resource pairs — which is what you would expect from
a regularizer. I have not measured any model-quality effect here and cannot: this day trains no model, and
quoting a number for how much it would help Akshara would be inventing one.

**BPE has an imitation of this and it is not the same mechanism.** BPE-dropout randomly skips merges during
encoding, producing varied segmentations from an ordered merge list. It is the `dropout` field Day 14 part
[3.1](../../../day-014-tiktoken-and-the-pipeline/parts/03-the-remaining-gap/3.1-the-file-is-the-contract.md)
found sitting in the model JSON. The effect is similar; the mechanism is skipping rules rather than
sampling a distribution, because there is no distribution in a merge list to sample.

**Determinism is a property you choose, not one you have.** That is the sentence to keep. A tokenizer is
deterministic *by configuration*, and Day 14's entire testing approach quietly assumed otherwise. Part 2.5
makes the collision explicit.

## Check yourself

```python
from collections import Counter
from pathlib import Path
from tokenizers import Tokenizer, models, pre_tokenizers, decoders, trainers

text = Path("docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md").read_text(encoding="utf-8")
ug = Tokenizer(models.Unigram())
ug.pre_tokenizer = pre_tokenizers.Metaspace()
ug.decoder = decoders.Metaspace()
ug.train_from_iterator([text], trainers.UnigramTrainer(
    vocab_size=1000, show_progress=False, special_tokens=["<unk>"], unk_token="<unk>"))

# 1. the default is deterministic
assert ug.model.nbest_size is None and ug.model.alpha is None
assert len({tuple(ug.encode("dollars").ids) for _ in range(20)}) == 1
print("sampling off: 20 encodings, 1 distinct result")

# 2. the eval that can go red: turning it on must produce variety
ug.model.nbest_size = 8
ug.model.alpha = 0.1
flat = Counter(tuple(ug.encode("dollars").tokens) for _ in range(400))
print(f"alpha=0.1: {len(flat)} distinct, argmax {flat.most_common(1)[0][1]}/400")
assert len(flat) > 1, "sampling produced one result - check nbest_size and alpha are both set"

# 3. alpha is a temperature: raising it must concentrate on the argmax
ug.model.alpha = 0.5
peaked = Counter(tuple(ug.encode("dollars").tokens) for _ in range(400))
top_flat = flat.most_common(1)[0][1]
top_peaked = peaked.most_common(1)[0][1]
print(f"alpha=0.5: {len(peaked)} distinct, argmax {top_peaked}/400")
assert top_peaked > top_flat, \
    f"alpha=0.5 did not concentrate more than 0.1 ({top_peaked} vs {top_flat}) - it is a sample, re-run once"

# 4. every sample is still correct
for p in ["plan", "The plan costs zero.", "Akshara"]:
    for _ in range(20):
        assert ug.decode(ug.encode(p).ids) == p
print("80 sampled encodings, 80 exact round trips - the round trip cannot see this")

# 5. the cost, measured
ug.model.alpha = 0.1
lengths = [len(ug.encode(text).ids) for _ in range(3)]
ug.model.nbest_size = None; ug.model.alpha = None
base = len(ug.encode(text).ids)
print(f"corpus tokens: deterministic {base}, sampled {lengths}")
print(f"overhead: {(sum(lengths)/3/base - 1) * 100:.0f}% more tokens")
assert len(set(lengths)) > 1, "three identical lengths - sampling is not active"
```

**Line by line:**

- Check 2 is the eval that can go red and it asserts *variety*, which is the opposite of what a tokenizer
  test normally asserts. Its message names the likely cause, because setting only one of the two knobs
  leaves sampling off and looks like it should work.
- Check 3 compares two sampled statistics, so it can fail by chance. The message says so and tells you to
  re-run once — an honest weakness stated in the failure text is better than a threshold chosen to hide it.
  For a real test suite, seed the generator or assert over more draws.
- Check 5 asserts the lengths **differ**. It is also the check that produces the number worth writing down:
  the token overhead you are paying for the regularization.
- Note check 5 restores `nbest_size = None` before measuring the baseline. Forgetting that restore is the
  production bug this part warns about, committed inside its own test.
