# 1.4 — 💥 The word that became one `[UNK]`

## One-line answer

`café` encodes to a single `[UNK]`, and `[UNK]` **decodes to the empty string** — so the word does not
become a placeholder, it *disappears* — and on the training corpus the `[UNK]` rate is **0.000%**, which is
exactly why nobody notices.

---

## The story

🎬 **The scene.** A form for a delivery address has a box for the house number and a box for the street.
Somebody lives at "Flat 2B". The number box accepts digits only.

😬 **The naive fix.** The form strips what it cannot parse and keeps going. `2B` becomes `2`.

💥 **Why it fails.** The parcel goes to flat 2. Nobody is told. The form did not reject the address, warn
about it, or record that anything was dropped — it produced a *valid-looking* address that is not where the
person lives. And for two years it worked perfectly, because almost everyone on the round lives at a
plain-numbered house.

💡 **The insight.** The dangerous failure is not the one that rejects your input. It is the one that
silently narrows your input to what the system can already handle, and reports success. The rate at which
it fires tells you nothing, because you only measured it on the addresses it was built from.

---

## The idea in plain language

Day 13 part
[3.3](../../../day-013-bpe-encode-decode-bytes/parts/03-byte-level-bpe/3.3-zero-out-of-vocabulary.md)
proved something strong: byte-level BPE has **zero out-of-vocabulary by construction**. Not "rarely" — never,
for any string in any script, because the 256 byte symbols cover every possible input.

WordPiece as classically configured gives that up, and this part measures what it costs. There are three
separate losses, and they compound:

1. **`[UNK]` takes the whole word**, not the offending character. Part
   [1.3](1.3-greedy-longest-match-first.md)'s `return [unk]` discards the pieces already matched.
2. **`[UNK]` decodes to nothing.** Not `<unk>`, not `?` — the empty string. The word is gone.
3. **The rate is zero on the corpus it was trained on**, so every measurement you are likely to take says
   the tokenizer is perfect.

The third is what makes the first two dangerous. This is **Silent Failure #5 — evaluated on the format you
trained on** — and it is the fourth day in a row it has appeared, which is the point of the plan listing it
as one of the five.

---

## Why Akshara needs it

**Because it decides the tokenizer family Akshara can use.** Akshara must *generate* text. A tokenizer that
cannot represent a character cannot generate it, ever, at any temperature. Day 16 picks the tokenizer that
goes into training, and this part is a hard constraint on that choice rather than a preference.

**Because the project is named in a script this destroys.** `अक्षर` — the word Akshara comes from — encodes
to a single `[UNK]` under this tokenizer and decodes to nothing at all. Part
[3.2](../../../day-014-tiktoken-and-the-pipeline/parts/03-the-remaining-gap/3.2-the-tax-a-vocabulary-charges-a-language.md)
of Day 14 measured that a general vocabulary charges Devanagari 6.88× too much. This is worse than
expensive: it is impossible.

**Because "0.000% unknown" is the shape of a lie you will meet again.** A metric measured on the
distribution that produced it. Every eval you build from Day 60 onwards has this failure available, and the
defence is always the same: hold out something *differently shaped*, not just something held out.

## The mechanism

### Step 1 — the rate on the training corpus

```python
UNK = wp.token_to_id("[UNK]")
out = wp.encode(text)
n_unk = sum(1 for i in out.ids if i == UNK)
print(f"{len(out.ids)} tokens, {n_unk} [UNK] = {n_unk / len(out.ids) * 100:.3f}%")
```

**Line by line:**

- `token_to_id("[UNK]")` looks the id up rather than assuming 0. It happens to *be* 0 here, and hard-coding
  it is the mistake Day 14 part
  [1.5](../../../day-014-tiktoken-and-the-pipeline/parts/01-tiktoken/1.5-the-special-token-that-was-just-text.md)
  warned about.
- Counting ids rather than tokens, because `[UNK]` is one id and comparing integers is exact.

Measured, corpus `9760f2b6d4340b97`:

```text
41735 tokens, 0 [UNK] = 0.000%
```

**Zero.** Not one unknown token in forty-one thousand. Every character in this corpus — including its
Devanagari, its em dashes, its emoji — is in the vocabulary, because the vocabulary was built from this
corpus. A report that stopped here would say the tokenizer is flawless.

### Step 2 — six words that are not in the corpus

```python
for probe in ["café", "योजना", "Россия", "😀", "naïve", "résumé"]:
    ids = wp.encode(probe).ids
    print(f"  {probe!r:12s} {len(probe):2d} chars -> {wp.encode(probe).tokens} "
          f"decode={wp.decode(ids)!r}")
```

**Line by line:**

- Six ordinary words. Two are English words with accents that any English corpus contains. One is Hindi,
  one Russian, one an emoji. None is exotic.
- Printing `len(probe)` alongside the token count makes the compression ratio visible, and it is about to
  be absurd.
- Printing the **decode** is the step people skip, and it is where the real damage is.

Measured:

```text
  'café'        4 chars -> ['[UNK]']  decode=''
  'योजना'       5 chars -> ['[UNK]']  decode=''
  'Россия'      6 chars -> ['[UNK]']  decode=''
  '😀'           1 chars -> ['[UNK]']  decode=''
  'naïve'       5 chars -> ['[UNK]']  decode=''
  'résumé'      6 chars -> ['[UNK]']  decode=''
```

**Six of six destroyed, and every one decodes to the empty string.**

Look at `café`. Three of its four characters — `c`, `a`, `f` — are certainly in the vocabulary. The `é` is
not. Greedy matching reaches position 3, finds nothing, and executes `return [unk]`, throwing away the
three pieces it had already matched. One character defeats the whole word.

Then decode returns `''`. Not `[UNK]`, not a placeholder a downstream system could notice — **nothing**.
The word is not marked as lost. It is simply absent, and the sentence around it closes up as though it had
never been written.

```python
print(repr(wp.decode(wp.encode("café").ids)))                          # ''
print(repr(wp.decode(wp.encode("café").ids, skip_special_tokens=False)))  # '[UNK]'
```

**Line by line:**

- The two calls differ only in `skip_special_tokens`, whose default is `True`. The information is not
  destroyed by `decode` — it is *hidden* by a default, and the second line recovers it.
- Day 14 part
  [3.1](../../../day-014-tiktoken-and-the-pipeline/parts/03-the-remaining-gap/3.1-the-file-is-the-contract.md)
  flagged this default as a debugging trap. Here it is the difference between a report that says a word was
  lost and one that says nothing happened.

`skip_special_tokens=True` is the default — Day 14 part
[3.1](../../../day-014-tiktoken-and-the-pipeline/parts/03-the-remaining-gap/3.1-the-file-is-the-contract.md)
flagged that default as a debugging trap and here is the consequence. The information exists; the default
throws it away.

### Step 3 — compare with what Day 13 built

```python
for probe in ["café", "योजना"]:
    print(f"  {probe!r:12s} -> {len(bpe13.tokenize(probe, merges))} tokens, exact round trip")
```

**Line by line:**

- `bpe13.tokenize` is your Day 13 module, unchanged, with the merge list trained on this same corpus at
  this same `V = 1000`. Nothing is being given an advantage.
- Printing the token count *and* asserting the round trip together is the point: the byte-level tokenizer
  is expensive here and it is not lossy, and those are separate facts about it.

```text
  'café'       -> 5 tokens, exact round trip
  'योजना'      -> 15 tokens, exact round trip
```

Your Day 13 byte-level tokenizer, trained on the *same* 113 KB corpus at the *same* `V = 1000`, handles both
perfectly. Fifteen tokens for a five-character Hindi word is expensive — that is Day 14 part 3.2's tax, and
it is the correct kind of bad. It is a word the model can read and can write. `[UNK]` is neither.

### Step 4 — the marker that collided with the data

There is a second, quieter loss in this tokenizer, and it is the hazard part
[1.2](1.2-the-hash-hash-convention.md) described as hypothetical, occurring for real on this corpus.

The WordPiece decoder joins tokens with spaces and then deletes every occurrence of `" ##"`. That rule
cannot distinguish the continuation marker from a `#` that is genuinely in the text — and this corpus is
markdown, full of `##` headings.

```python
for s in ["a ## b", "## Sub", "x ### y"]:
    print(f"  {s!r:10s} -> {wp.encode(s).tokens} -> {wp.decode(wp.encode(s).ids)!r}")
```

**Line by line:**

- Three probes differing only in position and length of the `#` run, which isolates the rule.

Measured:

```text
  'a ## b'   -> ['a', '##', 'b']    -> 'a b'
  '## Sub'   -> ['##', 'S', '##ub'] -> '## Sub'
  'x ### y'  -> ['x', '###', 'y']   -> 'x# y'
```

The **same token** `'##'` survives at the start of a string and vanishes in the middle, because only the
middle one is preceded by a space in the join. And `###` loses two of its three `#` for the same reason.

Across the whole corpus:

| token | occurrences | decoder emits |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| `'#'` | 52 | `'#'` |
| `'##'` | 31 | `''` |
| `'###'` | 25 | `'#'` |
| `'####'` | 37 | `'##'` |

**158 `#` characters destroyed** — measured by comparing character counts before and after. Every markdown
heading in the document is corrupted.

This is Day 14 part 1.5's lesson in a new costume: **a marker that shares a channel with data will
eventually be forged by the data.** `Ġ` cannot collide, because no real text contains U+0120. `##` collides
with every markdown file in the world.

## Shapes

| Name | Symbolic | `café` | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| input | `(n_chars,)` | `(4,)` | code points in |
| `ids` | `(T,)` | `(1,)` | one `[UNK]` |
| decoded | `(n_chars',)` | **`(0,)`** | the empty string |
| byte-level BPE `ids` | `(T,)` | `(5,)` | for comparison — and reversible |

`(4,) → (1,) → (0,)` is the whole part. Information does not merely compress; it reaches zero.

---

## When it breaks

**It does not raise. Ever.** `encode` succeeds, `decode` succeeds, the round trip returns a string. The
only symptom is that the string is missing words, and only for input unlike your training data.

**The corpus-level measurement hides it completely.** 0.000% on this corpus, 100% on six ordinary words.
Any check of the form "what fraction of my *training* tokens are unknown?" reports success. The check that
works is a **deliberately different** probe set:

```python
OOV_PROBES = ["café", "naïve", "Россия", "योजना", "😀", "日本語", "Ελλάδα"]
unk = wp.token_to_id("[UNK]")
for p in OOV_PROBES:
    ids = wp.encode(p).ids
    assert unk not in ids, f"{p!r} became [UNK] and will decode to {wp.decode(ids)!r}"
```

**Line by line:**

- The probes are chosen to be **absent from the corpus**, which is the whole design of the check. A probe
  list drawn from your training data measures nothing.
- The assertion message prints `wp.decode(ids)` — which will be `''` — so the failure report shows the
  disappearance rather than just naming the id. An error that displays the damage is worth the extra call.
- `unk not in ids` compares integers. Searching `wp.encode(p).tokens` for the string `[UNK]` is the check
  that looks equivalent and is not, for the reason part
  [2.1](../02-unigram/2.1-start-big-and-prune.md) measures.

**The `max_input_chars_per_word` guard adds a second silent path to `[UNK]`.** Part 1.3 measured it: a
101-character token becomes one `[UNK]` and therefore nothing. Base64 blobs, long URLs and minified code all
qualify, and all are common in real corpora.

**A model trained on this can never produce the missing characters.** This is the consequence that outlives
the tokenizer. If `é` is not in the vocabulary, no amount of training, prompting or sampling will make the
model write `café`. The ceiling was set before the first gradient step.

## In production

**This is why the family is used for encoders and not for generators.** Classification, retrieval and
tagging consume text and emit labels; a dropped accent degrades quality slightly. Generation emits text, and
a tokenizer that cannot represent a character is an absolute barrier. Match the tokenizer to what the model
must *produce* — the same conclusion part 1.2 reached from the whitespace side, arrived at independently.

**The repair exists and it is not `[UNK]`.** Part [3.2](../03-sentencepiece/3.2-byte-fallback.md) shows
`byte_fallback`: when a character has no token, emit its raw UTF-8 bytes as tokens instead of giving up.
That restores Day 13's guarantee to a non-byte-level tokenizer, and it is why modern SentencePiece
vocabularies carry `<0xC3>`-style entries. `[UNK]` is a design from before that idea was standard.

**Measure OOV on held-out, differently-shaped text, and put it in CI.** Not held-out from the same
distribution — *differently shaped*. Other scripts, other domains, machine-generated strings. The number you
want is not "how often does `[UNK]` fire on my data" but "what is the set of things my tokenizer cannot
represent", and that set is knowable in advance rather than sampled.

**A vocabulary is a ceiling on the model, and it is set on day one.** Everything downstream — the training
run, the fine-tune, the alignment, the serving stack — inherits it and cannot repair it. This is the
strongest single argument for the zero-OOV property, and it is why Day 13 spent a whole section proving it
by construction rather than by measurement.

## Check yourself

```python
from pathlib import Path
from tokenizers import Tokenizer, models, pre_tokenizers, decoders, trainers

text = Path("docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md").read_text(encoding="utf-8")
wp = Tokenizer(models.WordPiece(unk_token="[UNK]"))
wp.pre_tokenizer = pre_tokenizers.Whitespace()
wp.decoder = decoders.WordPiece()
wp.train_from_iterator([text], trainers.WordPieceTrainer(
    vocab_size=1000, show_progress=False, special_tokens=["[UNK]"]))
UNK = wp.token_to_id("[UNK]")

# 1. the reassuring number
ids = wp.encode(text).ids
rate = sum(1 for i in ids if i == UNK) / len(ids)
print(f"[UNK] rate on the training corpus: {rate * 100:.3f}%")
assert rate == 0.0, "expected zero - the vocabulary was built from this corpus"

# 2. the eval that can go red: ordinary words, deliberately not from the corpus
OOV = ["café", "naïve", "Россия", "योजना", "😀", "résumé"]
destroyed = [p for p in OOV if UNK in wp.encode(p).ids]
print(f"{len(destroyed)}/{len(OOV)} ordinary words become [UNK]")
for p in destroyed:
    assert wp.decode(wp.encode(p).ids) == "", \
        f"{p!r} decoded to something - check skip_special_tokens"
assert len(destroyed) == len(OOV), \
    "some probes survived - your corpus already contained them, so pick words it does not"

# 3. the marker/data collision, measured
from collections import Counter
hashes = Counter(t for t in wp.encode(text).tokens if set(t) == {"#"})
print("tokens made only of '#':", dict(hashes))
before = text.count("#")
after = wp.decode(wp.encode(text).ids).count("#")
print(f"'#' characters: {before} -> {after}  (lost {before - after})")
assert after < before, "no '#' lost - your corpus has no markdown headings"

# 4. and the comparison that makes the point
print("\nsame corpus, same V, byte-level BPE (Day 13):")
for p in ["café", "योजना"]:
    toks = bpe13.tokenize(p, merges)          # your Day 13 module
    assert bpe13.from_symbols(toks) == p, "byte-level round trip must be exact"
    print(f"  {p!r:12s} -> {len(toks)} tokens, exact")
```

**Line by line:**

- Block 1 asserts the *reassuring* number, which is unusual and deliberate. Pinning `rate == 0.0` records
  that the comforting metric genuinely is zero — so when block 2 fails, you cannot explain it away as a
  tokenizer that was broken all along.
- Block 2 is the eval that can go red. Note it asserts `decode(...) == ""`, not merely that `[UNK]` appears.
  The disappearance is the finding; the `[UNK]` id is only the mechanism.
- The message on the last assertion tells you what to do if it passes — your corpus contained the probes,
  so choose different ones. A probe set that has leaked into training measures nothing, which is Silent
  Failure #1 in miniature.
- Block 4 runs the same words through Day 13's tokenizer and asserts an exact round trip. Putting the
  working and broken tokenizers in the same test block is what turns a complaint into a comparison.
