# 1.2 — The `##` convention

## One-line answer

`##` means "this piece continues a word rather than starting one", so `cost` and `##s` are **different
tokens with different ids** — measured here, **444 of 1,000** vocabulary entries carry the prefix, and the
convention is what lets WordPiece drop the space and still reconstruct where words began.

---

## The story

You write a shopping list on a narrow notepad and a long word does not fit on one line.

You write `choco-` at the end of one line and `late` at the start of the next. The hyphen is not part of
the word. It is a mark that means *this is not finished — join it to what follows*.

Now imagine you left the hyphen off. Reading back, `choco` and `late` are two entries: buy some choco, buy
some late. The mark costs one character and it is the only thing standing between one item and two.

---

## The idea in plain language

Every tokenizer has to solve the same problem: after you split a sentence into pieces, how does the
detokenizer know where the spaces go?

You have now seen three answers, and this is the third:

| Approach | Where the space information lives | Day |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Byte-level BPE** | in the token: `Ġplan` *contains* the space | [13.3.2](../../../day-013-bpe-encode-decode-bytes/parts/03-byte-level-bpe/3.2-the-byte-to-unicode-trick.md) |
| **WordPiece `##`** | in a marker on the *continuation*: `plan` starts, `##s` continues | here |
| **SentencePiece `▁`** | in the token, like byte-level: `▁plan` | [3.1](../03-sentencepiece/3.1-the-metaspace-trick.md) |

The `##` approach is the odd one out, and the difference matters.

Byte-level BPE and SentencePiece mark the **space itself**. `Ġplan` and `▁plan` both say "there was a space
here, then `plan`". Whitespace is data.

WordPiece marks the **absence** of a space. `plan` means "a word starts here". `##s` means "no space before
this". So the tokenizer never represents whitespace at all — it represents *word boundaries*, and it
reconstructs a single space wherever one word ends and another begins.

That is why `cost` and `##s` must be two distinct vocabulary entries even though they share no characters
with each other: they are not two spellings, they are two *positions*. And it is why the round trip loses
information, which part [1.4](1.4-the-word-that-became-one-unk.md) measures.

---

## Why Akshara needs it

**Because it is a design decision you will have to make on Day 16**, when Akshara's tokenizer gets its
final form. Whether whitespace is data or a boundary marker determines whether your tokenizer can round-trip
code, markdown, or anything where two spaces differ from one.

**Because `##` is the visible signature of a whole model family.** Open a `vocab.txt` and see `##ing`,
and you know without reading further: WordPiece, whitespace pre-tokenizer, an `[UNK]` token somewhere, and a
detokenizer that will not give you your newlines back.

**Because it doubles the vocabulary pressure in a way that is easy to miss.** `s` and `##s` are two entries.
So are `ing` and `##ing`, `er` and `##er`. A vocabulary of 1,000 does not hold 1,000 distinct strings — the
measurement below shows how the budget is actually spent.

---

## The mechanism

### Step 1 — train one and look at the vocabulary

```python
from tokenizers import Tokenizer, models, pre_tokenizers, decoders, trainers

wp = Tokenizer(models.WordPiece(unk_token="[UNK]"))
wp.pre_tokenizer = pre_tokenizers.Whitespace()
wp.decoder = decoders.WordPiece()
wp.train_from_iterator([text], trainers.WordPieceTrainer(
    vocab_size=1000, show_progress=False, special_tokens=["[UNK]"]))

vocab = wp.get_vocab()
n_cont = sum(1 for t in vocab if t.startswith("##"))
print(f"vocabulary {len(vocab)}: {n_cont} continuation, {len(vocab) - n_cont} word-initial")
```

**Line by line:**

- `models.WordPiece(unk_token="[UNK]")` — the unknown token is a **constructor argument**, not optional
  configuration. WordPiece cannot function without one, which is the first sign that Day 13's zero-OOV
  guarantee does not apply here. Part 1.4 is the consequence.
- `pre_tokenizers.Whitespace()` splits on whitespace and word boundaries and **discards the whitespace**.
  This is the classic WordPiece configuration and it is where the losslessness goes; part 1.4 measures
  exactly what is lost.
- `decoders.WordPiece()` is the stage-5 inverse: strip `##`, concatenate, and insert a single space at
  every word boundary. Note what it cannot do — it does not know whether the original had one space, three
  spaces or a newline, so it always writes one space.
- `special_tokens=["[UNK]"]` puts `[UNK]` in the vocabulary at a known id. Without it the model has an
  `unk_token` that is not in its own vocabulary, and encoding an unknown word raises.

Measured, corpus `9760f2b6d4340b97`, `tokenizers==0.23.1`:

```text
vocabulary 1000: 444 continuation, 556 word-initial
```

**444 of 1,000 entries — 44.4% — are `##` forms.** That is the cost of the convention, and it is not small.

### Step 2 — the same letters, two entries

```python
for stem in ("s", "ing", "er", "the", "cost"):
    print(f"  {stem!r:6s} id={vocab.get(stem)}      {'##' + stem!r:8s} id={vocab.get('##' + stem)}")
```

**Line by line:**

- `vocab.get(...)` rather than `vocab[...]` so a missing entry prints `None` instead of raising. Both forms
  existing is common; only one existing is also common and is information.

Measured:

```text
  's'    id=80        '##s'    id=156
  'ing'  id=None      '##ing'  id=245
  'er'   id=None      '##er'   id=238
  'the'  id=241       '##the'  id=None
  'cost' id=941       '##cost' id=None
```

Read those five rows carefully, because they are the convention working exactly as intended:

- **`s` and `##s` both exist**, at completely unrelated ids. `s` is the word "s" (rare, but it occurs);
  `##s` is the plural suffix (extremely common). Two ids, two meanings.
- **`ing` and `er` exist only as `##` forms.** No English word is "ing", so the trainer never needed a
  word-initial entry, and did not waste a slot on one.
- **`the` and `cost` exist only as word-initial forms.** They are words, not suffixes.

So the vocabulary is not paying double for everything. Measured, only **136 of the 1,000 entries** are
strings that exist in *both* positions — and inspecting them, the first several are punctuation (`"`, `#`,
`'`, `(`, `)`, `*`), which genuinely does occur both at the start of a chunk and inside one. The other
308 continuation entries are suffix-only, like `##ing`. The 44.4% is real; the duplication is 13.6%.

### Step 3 — watch it segment

```python
print(wp.encode("The plan costs zero dollars.").tokens)
```

```text
['The', 'plan', 'cost', '##s', 'zero', 'do', '##l', '##l', '##ar', '##s', '.']
```

**Line by line:**

- `The`, `plan`, `zero` and `.` are word-initial and complete — the vocabulary has whole tokens for them.
- `cost` + `##s` is the convention paying off. The tokenizer found the stem and marked the suffix as a
  continuation, so a model sees "the word *cost*, pluralized" rather than an opaque `costs`. This is the
  morphology Day 11 part
  [2.4](../../../day-011-character-and-word-tokenizers/parts/02-word-level/2.4-one-meaning-six-slots.md)
  measured the word tokenizer failing to capture.
- `do` + `##l` + `##l` + `##ar` + `##s` is five tokens for one common word, and it is ugly. `dollars` did
  not appear often enough in a 113 KB markdown plan to earn a better segmentation. That is a corpus
  problem, not an algorithm problem — part [1.4](1.4-the-word-that-became-one-unk.md)'s wider point.
- Every `##` in the output is a place the decoder will **not** insert a space. Count them and you can
  predict the reconstruction exactly.

### Step 4 — the decoder undoes it

```python
ids = wp.encode("The plan costs zero dollars.").ids
print(repr(wp.decode(ids)))
```

**Line by line:**

- `wp.decode(ids)` runs stage 5: strip every `##`, join the rest, and insert a single space at each word
  boundary. Count the `##` tokens in the output above and you can predict this line's result exactly — five
  continuations, so five joins without a space and six with one.
- Passing `ids` rather than tokens, because the decoder is defined over ids. Feeding it token strings is
  the `DecodePieces` trap part [3.2](../03-sentencepiece/3.2-byte-fallback.md) measures in another family.

```text
'The plan costs zero dollars.'
```

Correct — on this string. Every word boundary got exactly one space, and the original had exactly one
space at every word boundary. Part 1.4 is what happens when that stops being true.

---

## Shapes

| Name | Symbolic | Concrete | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `vocab` | `(V,)` | `(1000,)` | token → id, including `[UNK]` |
| word-initial entries | `(V_start,)` | `(556,)` | may begin a word |
| continuation entries | `(V_cont,)` | `(444,)` | may only follow, marked `##` |
| `ids` for the probe | `(T,)` | `(11,)` | 11 tokens for 28 characters |

`V_start + V_cont = V` exactly — every entry is one or the other, and `[UNK]` counts as word-initial.

---

## When it breaks

**`##` is not reserved, and a corpus can contain it.** Markdown headings, C preprocessor tokens and shell
comments all use `#`. If a chunk in your corpus literally is `##s`, the vocabulary cannot distinguish "the
literal string `##s`" from "the suffix `s`". Nothing raises; you simply have one entry meaning two things.
This is the same class of problem as Day 14 part
[1.5](../../../day-014-tiktoken-and-the-pipeline/parts/01-tiktoken/1.5-the-special-token-that-was-just-text.md)'s
special token — a marker sharing a channel with data — and the byte-level `Ġ` convention avoids it
entirely, because `Ġ` is a character no real text contains.

**The decoder always writes exactly one space.** Feed it a document with double spaces, tabs or newlines
and you get single spaces back:

```text
'a  b'         -> ['a', 'b']              -> 'a b'
'line1\nline2' -> ['line', '##1', 'line', '##2'] -> 'line1 line2'
'  indented'   -> ['in', '##d', '##ent', '##ed'] -> 'indented'
```

No error, no warning, and the round trip is `False` for all three. Part 1.4 measures this across the whole
corpus.

**A `##` token at the start of a sequence is meaningless and possible.** Nothing prevents a model from
generating `##ing` as its first token, and nothing in the decoder complains — it just strips the prefix.
Generation code that assembles output token by token has to handle it.

**Confusing the prefix with the vocabulary size.** A `V = 30000` WordPiece vocabulary does not hold 30,000
distinct strings. On this corpus 44.4% of entries are `##` duplicates of positions rather than of strings;
the count of distinct character sequences is meaningfully lower than `V`.

## In production

**`##` is a convention, not a standard.** Its exact spelling is a parameter — `continuing_subword_prefix`
in the `tokenizers` file, which Day 14 part
[3.1](../../../day-014-tiktoken-and-the-pipeline/parts/03-the-remaining-gap/3.1-the-file-is-the-contract.md)
listed among the `model` flags you never set. Change it and every id changes. It is exactly the kind of
setting that must travel in the file rather than in somebody's memory.

**The whitespace loss is why this family is rare for generative models.** A model that cannot reproduce
newlines cannot write code, markdown, or poetry. WordPiece is common in encoder models — classification,
retrieval, tagging — where the output is a label rather than text, and the input's exact whitespace does
not matter. Match the tokenizer to what the model must *produce*.

**`##` makes vocabularies human-auditable in a way byte-level does not.** Day 13 part
[3.4](../../../day-013-bpe-encode-decode-bytes/parts/03-byte-level-bpe/3.4-the-vocabulary-you-cannot-read.md)
showed a byte-level vocabulary full of `Ġâ` and `Ģ` that no reviewer can read. A WordPiece vocabulary is a
list of readable words and suffixes. That is a genuine operational advantage — you can eyeball it for
contamination, for slurs, for private data — and it is one of the few things this family does better.

**The morphology it exposes is real but not automatic.** `cost`/`##s` is a clean split; `do`/`##l`/`##l`/
`##ar`/`##s` is not. Whether a vocabulary carves at morpheme boundaries depends on corpus size, not on the
`##` convention, and claiming WordPiece "understands morphology" overstates what the measurement supports.

## Check yourself

```python
from tokenizers import Tokenizer, models, pre_tokenizers, decoders, trainers
from pathlib import Path

text = Path("docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md").read_text(encoding="utf-8")
wp = Tokenizer(models.WordPiece(unk_token="[UNK]"))
wp.pre_tokenizer = pre_tokenizers.Whitespace()
wp.decoder = decoders.WordPiece()
wp.train_from_iterator([text], trainers.WordPieceTrainer(
    vocab_size=1000, show_progress=False, special_tokens=["[UNK]"]))

vocab = wp.get_vocab()
cont = {t for t in vocab if t.startswith("##")}
print(f"{len(cont)} continuation / {len(vocab)} total = {len(cont)/len(vocab)*100:.1f}%")

# every entry is exactly one of the two kinds
assert len(cont) + len(vocab) - len(cont) == len(vocab)

# the eval that can go red: a token and its ## form must be DIFFERENT ids
both = [t for t in vocab if not t.startswith("##") and "##" + t in vocab]
print(f"{len(both)} strings that exist in both positions, e.g. {sorted(both)[:6]}")
for t in both:
    assert vocab[t] != vocab["##" + t], f"{t!r} and '##{t}' share an id - the convention is broken"

# no continuation token may start a decoded string with a space
probe = "The plan costs zero dollars."
out = wp.encode(probe)
n_cont_used = sum(1 for t in out.tokens if t.startswith("##"))
print(f"{len(out.tokens)} tokens, {n_cont_used} continuations -> "
      f"{len(out.tokens) - n_cont_used} word boundaries")
assert wp.decode(out.ids) == probe, "single-spaced ASCII should round-trip"

# and the failure, asserted deliberately
for lossy in ["a  b", "line1\nline2", "  indented"]:
    assert wp.decode(wp.encode(lossy).ids) != lossy, \
        f"{lossy!r} round-tripped - your pre-tokenizer is keeping whitespace, unlike the classic setup"
print("whitespace loss confirmed on all three probes")
```

**Line by line:**

- The `vocab[t] != vocab["##" + t]` loop is the eval that can go red, and it tests the *claim* of this part:
  the two positions are two tokens. If a version ever collapsed them, every downstream assumption about
  word boundaries would silently change.
- `both` is computed rather than hard-coded so the test works on any corpus — and printing its length tells
  you how much of the 44.4% is genuine duplication of strings rather than suffix-only entries.
- The last loop asserts three round trips **fail**. Third day running that the useful assertion is a
  negative one; by now that should feel normal rather than strange.
