# 4.1 — Nine tokenizers, one table

## One-line answer

Five days of building put nine tokenizers on one corpus; only **three** are both lossless on the corpus
and complete on unseen scripts, all three represent text as bytes somewhere, and the only one of them at
`V = 1000` — SentencePiece with `identity` and `byte_fallback` — is also the **worst compressor** in the
table at 2.195 chars/token.

---

## The story

You have been trying nine different bags for the same trip.

The lightest one does not close properly. The one that holds everything is the heaviest. Two of them look
identical until you notice one has no inside pocket.

Laid out on the floor together, the choice finally looks like what it is — not "which is best" but "which
thing am I willing to be worst at". Until they were side by side, every bag looked fine on its own.

---

## The idea in plain language

Day 14 part
[4.1](../../../day-014-tiktoken-and-the-pipeline/parts/04-together/4.1-six-tokenizers-one-table.md)
made a table of six and drew one conclusion: the best compressor was the one you could not use, because
compression was never the property that mattered.

Three more rows arrive today, and they make a sharper point. The table now has **two** veto columns, not
one:

- **Lossless?** — does `decode(encode(corpus))` return the corpus exactly?
- **Complete?** — can it represent text it has never seen?

These are different questions and today's parts kept demonstrating that they come apart. Unigram is
lossless on this corpus and destroys `café`. SentencePiece with byte fallback handles `café` and loses
1,646 newlines. **A tokenizer can pass either check and fail the other**, and until you measure both you
do not know which one you have.

---

## Why Akshara needs it

**Because Day 16 picks one and cannot change its mind.** The vocabulary is frozen before training and its
ids are baked into a `(V, C)` embedding matrix. This is the last day before that choice, and the table is
the evidence for it.

**Because the ledger is the memory.** The plan says 162 days is long enough that Day 30 forgets Day 11.
Nine rows, one corpus hash, every number measured on this machine — that is what survives.

**Because Day 17 breaks tokenizers deliberately** (`TOK-18`, `TOK-19`, `TOK-20`) and needs a baseline to
measure the damage against.

---

## The mechanism

All nine on `docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md`, sha256 `9760f2b6d4340b97`, 113,283 bytes, 110,837 code points.

```python
import hashlib
from pathlib import Path

P = Path("docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md")
text = P.read_text(encoding="utf-8")
print("corpus", hashlib.sha256(P.read_bytes()).hexdigest()[:16], len(text), "chars")

OOV = ["café", "योजना", "😀"]     # none of these is exotic; all are absent or rare here
```

**Line by line:**

- One `text` variable for all nine, as Day 13 and Day 14 established. That rule is what makes this a
  comparison rather than nine facts.
- `OOV` is the second measurement, and it is the one Day 14's table lacked. Three ordinary words: a French
  loan word, a Hindi word, an emoji. **The completeness column is meaningless without probes the corpus
  does not contain**, which is Silent Failure #5 stated as a test-design rule.

### The table

| # | Tokenizer | `V` | tokens | chars/token | 1,024-win | lossless? | of 3 OOV probes |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | --- | --- |
| 1 | character (D11) | 151 | 110,837 | 1.000 | 1,024 | ✅ | 3 lost |
| 2 | word (D11) | 3,118 | 29,081 | **3.811** | **3,903** | ❌ | `UNK` |
| 3 | char BPE (D12) | 1,000 | 42,130 | 2.631 | 2,694 | ✅ | fails |
| 4 | byte BPE (D13) | 1,000 | 46,311 | 2.393 | 2,451 | ✅ | **0 lost** |
| 5 | `tiktoken` `gpt2` (D14) | 50,257 | 33,405 | 3.318 | 3,398 | ✅ | **0 lost** |
| 6 | WordPiece (D15) | 1,000 | 41,735 | 2.656 | 2,719 | ❌ | **3 lost** |
| 7 | Unigram (D15) | 1,000 | 43,038 | 2.575 | 2,637 | ✅ | **3 lost** |
| 8 | SentencePiece, defaults (D15) | 1,000 | 46,770 | 2.370 | 2,427 | ❌ | 0 lost |
| 9 | **SentencePiece, `identity` + `byte_fallback`** | 1,000 | 50,493 | **2.195** | 2,248 | ✅ | **0 lost** |

Row 2's word rule is `\w+|[^\w\s]`; row 3 is Day 12's measurement carried forward.

### Reading it

**Three of the nine pass both vetoes: rows 4, 5 and 9.** Rows 4 and 5 are byte-level BPE, and row 5 buys
its compression with 50× the vocabulary. Among the `V = 1000` rows, **exactly one** is both lossless and
complete — row 9.

**And row 9 is the worst compressor in the table.** 2.195 chars/token, below every other `V = 1000` row and
below even the character-BPE of Day 12. That is the cost, itemized:

- **256 slots to byte fallback** (part [3.2](../03-sentencepiece/3.2-byte-fallback.md)) — 25.6% of the
  budget, measured at +11.2% tokens.
- **`identity` normalization** (part [3.3](../03-sentencepiece/3.3-the-normalizer-that-was-already-on.md))
  — newlines and `²` now need vocabulary entries instead of being folded away.

**The two vetoes genuinely come apart, and rows 7 and 8 prove it.**

- **Row 7, Unigram: lossless ✅, complete ❌.** It round-trips all 110,837 characters and destroys all three
  OOV probes. Its coverage is a *measurement* over the training corpus.
- **Row 8, SentencePiece defaults: lossless ❌, complete ✅.** Byte fallback handles every probe; the
  default normalizer eats 1,646 newlines before tokenization begins.

Two tokenizers, opposite failures, and a table with only one veto column would have called one of them
fine.

**Row 6 is the day's worst outcome and it took two independent flaws to get there.** WordPiece fails both:
the whitespace pre-tokenizer loses newlines and the `##` marker collides with markdown (part
[1.4](../01-wordpiece/1.4-the-word-that-became-one-unk.md)), while `[UNK]` swallows whole words and decodes
to the empty string. Note it also compresses better than rows 7, 8 and 9 — a reminder of Day 14's
conclusion that the compression column has no vote.

**Rows 6 and 7 are the same corpus, the same `V`, and 3.0% apart on compression.** WordPiece 41,735,
Unigram 43,038. Two genuinely different algorithms — greedy longest-match against Viterbi over a
probability model — separated by three percent. Whatever decides between these families, it is not this
column.

## Shapes

| Name | Symbolic | Range across the nine | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `V` | scalar | 151 → 50,257 | embedding rows; `(V, C)` parameters |
| `T` for this corpus | `(T,)` | 29,081 → 110,837 | **3.8×** for the same text |
| chars per 1,024 window | scalar | 1,024 → 3,903 | how much text the model sees |
| lossless rows | scalar | 6 / 9 | rows 1, 3, 4, 5, 7, 9 |
| complete rows | scalar | 4 / 9 | rows 4, 5, 8, 9 |
| **both** | scalar | **3 / 9** | rows 4, 5, 9 — all byte-based |

The **both** row is the summary of five days: three tokenizers out of nine are safe to generate with, and
every one of them represents text as bytes somewhere. Row 4 and row 5 are byte-level from the alphabet up
(Day 13); row 9 is character-based with a byte escape hatch (part
[3.2](../03-sentencepiece/3.2-byte-fallback.md)). There is no fourth route.

Row 1, the character tokenizer, is **not** among them, which is worth checking rather than assuming: its
alphabet is the 151 characters of *this corpus*, so `é`, Devanagari and emoji are all outside it. It is
lossless on the corpus and incomplete off it — the same pair as row 7, reached by a much simpler route. And
it would be unusable anyway, for the reason Day 11 part
[1.3](../../../day-011-character-and-word-tokenizers/parts/01-character-level/1.3-the-sequence-length-problem.md)
measured: at 1.000 chars/token it costs 14.5× the attention work of row 2 on the same document.

---

## When it breaks

**Ranking on compression.** Row 2 wins it and is lossy. Row 9 loses it and is the only `V = 1000` row that
passes both vetoes. The column is real and it has no vote until the vetoes are satisfied.

**Testing completeness on your training corpus.** Row 7 reports zero unknowns on this corpus and destroys
three of three ordinary words. Any measurement of coverage taken over the data that built the vocabulary
is a measurement of nothing — which is exactly why the `OOV` list is defined next to `text` in the script.

**Comparing rows measured on different corpora.** Every number here comes from one file with a printed
hash. A table whose rows come from different corpus versions is a coincidence, not a comparison.

**Reading row 9 as "the answer".** It is the answer *at `V = 1000` on 113 KB of English markdown*. At
`V = 32000` the 256 byte-fallback slots stop mattering and the compression penalty largely disappears —
which is why this configuration is standard in production and looks expensive here. The table's shape at
toy scale exaggerates the one cost you would not pay for real.

---

## In production

**Regenerate this table; do not remember it.** A script that rebuilds all nine from the corpus, prints the
hash and fails on a regression is worth more than the numbers written once. When a tokenizer changes, the
diff is the review.

**The real choice needs columns this corpus cannot fill.** Multilingual rate — Day 14 part
[3.2](../../../day-014-tiktoken-and-the-pipeline/parts/03-the-remaining-gap/3.2-the-tax-a-vocabulary-charges-a-language.md)
began it. Code, numbers, indentation. And the question none of these columns touch: does a model trained
with this vocabulary actually do better? That one costs a training run per vocabulary to answer, which is
why the proxies matter and why they should be chosen carefully.

**`V` is a parameter budget.** At `C = 768`, row 5's 50,257 vocabulary is 38.6M embedding parameters plus
the same again on the output projection. For a small model that can exceed everything else combined —
Day 26 (`EFF-01`) makes you do the arithmetic properly, and Day 17 (`TOK-20`) makes you choose against it.

**Nobody trains a vocabulary on 113 KB.** Day 14 part
[1.4](../../../day-014-tiktoken-and-the-pipeline/parts/01-tiktoken/1.4-your-merges-against-theirs.md)
measured 22.4% of a merge table going to markdown formatting rather than language. Every row here shares
that limitation. The table compares *implementations* honestly and tells you nothing about what any of them
would do on a real corpus.

## Check yourself

```python
import hashlib
from pathlib import Path

P = Path("docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md")
text = P.read_text(encoding="utf-8")
print("corpus", hashlib.sha256(P.read_bytes()).hexdigest()[:16])

OOV = ["café", "योजना", "😀"]
for p in OOV:
    assert p not in text or text.count(p) < 3, \
        f"{p!r} is common in your corpus - pick probes it does not contain"

# rows: (name, V, n_tokens, lossless, n_oov_lost)
rows = []          # ... fill from your own Day 11-15 code ...

print(f"{'tokenizer':24s} {'V':>7s} {'tokens':>8s} {'ch/tok':>7s} {'lossless':>9s} {'OOV lost':>9s}")
for name, V, n, lossless, lost in rows:
    print(f"{name:24s} {V:7d} {n:8d} {len(text)/n:7.3f} {str(lossless):>9s} {lost:>9d}")

# 1. the eval that can go red: the best compressor must not be the recommendation
best = max(rows, key=lambda r: len(text) / r[2])
assert not (best[3] and best[4] == 0), \
    f"{best[0]} wins compression AND passes both vetoes - say so, it is a real finding"

# 2. the two vetoes must be independent - find a row that passes each and fails the other
lossless_only = [r for r in rows if r[3] and r[4] > 0]
complete_only = [r for r in rows if not r[3] and r[4] == 0]
print("lossless but incomplete:", [r[0] for r in lossless_only])
print("complete but lossy     :", [r[0] for r in complete_only])
assert lossless_only and complete_only, \
    "no row separates the two vetoes - then one column would have done, and this part is wrong"

# 3. exactly one V=1000 row should pass both
both = [r for r in rows if r[3] and r[4] == 0 and r[1] == 1000]
print("V=1000 rows passing both:", [r[0] for r in both])
assert len(both) == 1, f"expected exactly one, got {[r[0] for r in both]}"

# 4. and it should be the worst compressor of the V=1000 rows
small = [r for r in rows if r[1] == 1000]
worst = min(small, key=lambda r: len(text) / r[2])
assert worst[0] == both[0][0], \
    f"the complete-and-lossless row is not the worst compressor ({worst[0]}) - report what you measured"
print(f"{both[0][0]} passes both vetoes and compresses worst - that is the trade")
```

**Line by line:**

- `rows` is left for you to fill, because assembling nine numbers from five days of your own code is the
  exercise. Copying my table defeats it, and Principle 8 says the numbers must be yours.
- Check 1 is Day 14's assertion carried forward, now with the second veto folded in. Its message allows for
  the possibility that on *your* corpus the best compressor does pass both — in which case that is a
  finding to report, not a test to force.
- Check 2 is the eval that can go red and it is the one carrying this part's argument. If no row separates
  the two vetoes then a single column would have sufficed and the whole framing is wrong. Asserting that
  the distinction is *load-bearing* is stronger than asserting any particular row's value.
- Checks 3 and 4 pin the closing result and its cost together. Separating them would let you report the
  good news without the price.
