# 2.2 — Where the tokenizer sits: both ends of the stack, and only the ends

## One-line answer

The tokenizer is the **first** thing text meets and the **last** thing ids pass through, and nothing
in between ever sees a character — so the entire model, from the embedding table to the loss, operates
on integers whose meaning lives in a table the model does not contain.

---

## The story

You are at a doctor's appointment with an interpreter, because you and the doctor do not share a
language.

The interpreter sits at both ends of the conversation. Your words go in, English comes out to the
doctor. The doctor's reply goes in, your language comes out to you. In between those two moments the
interpreter does nothing at all — the doctor examines, thinks and decides entirely in English, and
never learns a word of your language for as long as you both live.

That is the arrangement, and it works. But notice what it depends on.

It has to be the **same interpreter at both ends**. If somebody else takes over for the reply — a
different person, with different habits, who renders the same words slightly differently — then the
answer you hear is not the answer the doctor gave. Nobody in the room will notice, because the doctor
hears fluent English going out and you hear fluent speech coming back, and neither of you can check
the other half.

And the interpreter is not part of the doctor. If the doctor moves to another clinic, the interpreter
does not automatically come along.

---

## The idea in plain language

Part [2.1](2.1-a-function-and-its-inverse.md) established what a tokenizer *is*. This part is about
where it sits, which is a shorter idea and an easier one to get wrong.

The full path a piece of text takes, from a prompt to a reply:

1. **Text arrives.** A string. Characters, spaces, newlines.
2. **`encode`.** The tokenizer turns it into a list of integers. This is the last moment any character
   exists.
3. **Batching.** Those ids are stacked into a `(B, T)` integer tensor — `B` sequences of `T` tokens.
4. **The embedding table.** Each id is used as a **row index** into a `(V, C)` matrix, producing a
   `(B, T, C)` tensor of vectors. This is where an integer becomes something with a direction and a
   magnitude.
5. **The model.** Attention, feed-forward blocks, normalisation — all of Days 25 to 39. Shapes change,
   numbers change, and none of it knows what any of it means.
6. **The output head.** A `(B, T, C)` tensor becomes `(B, T, V)` logits — one score per vocabulary
   entry per position, from Day 5.
7. **Sampling.** Softmax and a draw, from Day 5 part
   [2.1](../../../day-005-logits-softmax-sampling/parts/02-sampling/2.1-sampling-from-a-categorical.md),
   produce one integer.
8. **`decode`.** The tokenizer turns the integers back into text. This is the first moment a character
   exists again.

Steps 2 and 8 are the tokenizer. Steps 3 to 7 are the model. **There is no step in the middle where
the tokenizer is consulted**, and that is the fact worth carrying: a running model cannot ask "what
does token 4,271 mean?", because it has no access to anything that could answer.

Two consequences fall straight out of that, and both are load-bearing:

**The embedding table is where the tokenizer touches the model.** Row `i` of the `(V, C)` embedding
matrix is *the* vector for token `i`, and which piece of text token `i` is depends entirely on the
tokenizer's table. Change the table and you have not changed the model's weights at all — you have
changed what every one of its rows means. Part [2.5](2.5-the-tokenizer-that-did-not-match.md) is that
sentence, demonstrated.

**`T` is decided outside the model.** The sequence length is whatever `encode` produced. A model with a
1,024-token context does not hold 1,024 words or 1,024 characters; it holds 1,024 *of whatever this
tokenizer emits*, which is why part [1.3](../01-the-vocabulary-problem/1.3-why-not-characters.md)'s
length ratio is a capability question rather than a speed question.

## Why Akshara needs it

- **Day 26** builds the embedding table, and its whole job is "use an integer as a row index". This
  part is why that operation is the seam between two systems rather than just another layer.
- **Day 39** assembles Akshara v0 and counts its parameters. The embedding and output matrices are
  sized `V × C` each, and `V` came from a decision made on this day, in a different file, by a
  different piece of code.
- **Day 51 onward**, every training batch is a `(B, T)` integer tensor produced by the data loader,
  which calls `encode` and nothing else. Everything the plan's §6 says about tokenizer/template
  mismatch (Silent Failure #2) lives in the gap between the `encode` the loader calls and the `encode`
  the serving path calls.
- **Day 100 onward**, the HTTP surface receives strings and returns strings, so steps 1–2 and 8 are
  literally the request and response handlers. A serving bug in those two functions is
  indistinguishable, from the outside, from a bad model.

## The mechanism

The whole path, with the tokenizer at both ends and a deliberately trivial "model" in the middle, so
that the seams are the only thing on the page.

```python
import pathlib
import random

text = pathlib.Path("docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md").read_text(encoding="utf-8")
chars = sorted(set(text))
stoi = {ch: i for i, ch in enumerate(chars)}
itos = {i: ch for ch, i in stoi.items()}
V = len(stoi)


def encode(s: str) -> list[int]:
    return [stoi[ch] for ch in s]


def decode(ids: list[int]) -> str:
    return "".join(itos[i] for i in ids)


def fake_model(ids: list[int], rng: random.Random) -> int:
    """Stands in for Days 25-39. Sees integers, returns an integer, knows nothing else."""
    assert all(0 <= i < V for i in ids), "an id outside [0, V) cannot index the embedding table"
    return rng.randrange(V)                       # a real model would produce V logits here
```

**Line by line:**
- `fake_model` takes `list[int]` and returns `int`. **Its type signature is the whole point of this
  part**: there is no `str` anywhere in it, and there is no way to put one in without going back
  through `decode`.
- The `assert` is the one real check a model can make about its input, and it is a range check, not a
  meaning check. `0 <= i < V` is exactly what the embedding table can service; anything else is an
  `IndexError` at best. It cannot check that the ids came from the *right* tokenizer, because nothing
  in an integer records that.
- `rng` is passed in rather than read from the global `random` module. Day 5 part
  [2.3](../../../day-005-logits-softmax-sampling/parts/02-sampling/2.3-seeds-and-generators.md)
  established why: a function whose output depends on an unseeded global is not reproducible, and the
  fix is to make the generator an argument.
- `rng.randrange(V)` is where a real model would apply softmax to `V` logits and sample. The shape of
  the interface is identical; only the quality of the choice differs.

Now walk one string all the way through and print the type at every step:

```python
rng = random.Random(1337)
prompt = "Overfit one batch"

ids = encode(prompt)                              # (T,)
print(f"  1. text   {type(prompt).__name__:12} {prompt!r}")
print(f"  2. encode {type(ids).__name__:12} {ids[:6]}...  T = {len(ids)}")

batch = [ids, encode("A model that")]             # (B, T) once padded — Day 51
print(f"  3. batch  {type(batch).__name__:12} B = {len(batch)}, lengths {[len(x) for x in batch]}")

next_id = fake_model(ids, rng)                    # ()
print(f"  4-7. model {type(next_id).__name__:12} {next_id}")

out = decode(ids + [next_id])                     # str
print(f"  8. decode {type(out).__name__:12} {out!r}")
```

**Line by line:**
- Read the type column downwards: `str`, `list`, `list`, `int`, `str`. **Text at the top, text at the
  bottom, integers everywhere in between.** That column is the whole lesson.
- `batch` is two sequences of different lengths, which is the honest picture: real text does not arrive
  in equal lengths, and making it rectangular is padding — Day 6 part
  [2.5](../../../day-006-entropy-cross-entropy-perplexity/parts/02-cross-entropy/2.5-the-loss-that-counted-padding.md)
  is what happens when the padding is then counted in the loss.
- `ids + [next_id]` is generation in one line: the model's output is appended to its own input, and the
  next call sees a sequence one longer. Day 69's KV cache exists because this line, done naively,
  re-reads the whole prefix every time.
- Measured on Intel Core i3-1115G4 (2 cores / 4 threads), 11.7 GB RAM, Windows 11, CPython 3.12.12, on
  2026-08-26, with `random.Random(1337)`, against `docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md` at the revision in this
  repository:

```text
  1. text   str          'Overfit one batch'
  2. encode list         [47, 84, 67, 80, 68, 71]...  T = 17
  3. batch  list         B = 2, lengths [17, 12]
  4-7. model int          136
  8. decode str          'Overfit one batch🔍'
```

The magnifying glass on the end is a random token, because `fake_model` is random. Id `136` is a
**real** entry in a **real** vocabulary — this corpus contains emoji, so the vocabulary does too — and
`decode` rendered it without hesitation. That is the point: whatever the model produces, `decode` will
render it as text, and **text always looks like text**.

The path, drawn once:

```mermaid

    T1["text<br/>str"] -->|encode| I1["ids<br/>(T,)"]
    I1 -->|batch + pad| B["(B, T)<br/>int"]

    E -->|"blocks<br/>(Days 25-39)"| H["(B, T, C)<br/>float"]

    style T1 fill:#dfd

```

The two green boxes are the only places a character exists. The two yellow boxes are the tokenizer. Everything
else is arithmetic on numbers that mean nothing on their own.

## Shapes

| Tensor | Symbolic shape | Concrete here | What each axis means |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `prompt` | — | 17 characters | a Python `str`; no axes, not a tensor |
| `ids` | `(T,)` | `(17,)` | one integer per token; `T` is decided by `encode` |
| batch of ids | `(B, T)` | `(2, 17)` after padding | `B` sequences · `T` positions |
| embedding table | `(V, C)` | `(150, C)` | one row per vocabulary entry · `C` channels |
| embedded batch | `(B, T, C)` | `(2, 17, C)` | batch · position · channel |
| logits | `(B, T, V)` | `(2, 17, 150)` | batch · position · **one score per vocabulary entry** |
| sampled id | `()` | scalar | one integer, back at the tokenizer's door |

Read the `V` in two rows: it is the **first** axis of the embedding table and the **last** axis of the
logits, and it is the same `V` both times. That is the model's entire connection to the tokenizer —
two matrices whose size is a decision made in a different file.

## When it breaks

**The loud failure — an id outside the vocabulary.** The embedding table is indexed by id, so an id of
`V` or more is an index error, not a bad prediction:

```console
>>> E = [[0.0] * 8 for _ in range(150)]     # a (V, C) embedding table, in plain Python
>>> E[150]
Traceback (most recent call last):
  File "<stdin>", line 1, in <module>
IndexError: list index out of range
```

A tensor library raises the same class of error with a more informative message — it knows the axis
and the size — but the cause is identical, and the plain-Python version is the one this part can run
with no dependency at all. What it means: something upstream produced an id this vocabulary does not
have. In practice that is
almost always a **tokenizer whose `V` does not match the model's `V`** — a vocabulary that was extended,
or a special token added on one side only. Day 16's vocabulary surgery is the careful version of that
operation, and this traceback is what the careless version looks like.

**The silent failure — the ids are in range and mean something else.** Nothing raises, because every id
is a valid row index. The model embeds them, attends over them, and produces fluent output about a
different sentence. Part [2.5](2.5-the-tokenizer-that-did-not-match.md) reproduces this in full.

**The check that catches both, at the seam:**

```python
def check_ids(ids: list[int], V: int, tokenizer_hash: str, model_tokenizer_hash: str) -> None:
    """Two checks at the boundary: the ids fit, and they came from the right table."""
    bad = [i for i in ids if not 0 <= i < V]
    if bad:
        raise ValueError(f"{len(bad)} id(s) outside [0, {V}): {bad[:5]}")
    if tokenizer_hash != model_tokenizer_hash:
        raise ValueError(
            f"tokenizer mismatch: ids were produced by {tokenizer_hash[:12]} "
            f"but this model was trained with {model_tokenizer_hash[:12]}"
        )
```

**Line by line:**
- The range check is cheap and catches the loud case *before* it becomes an `IndexError` several layers
  deeper, where the traceback points at the embedding lookup rather than at the tokenizer.
- The hash check is the only thing that catches the silent case. **An integer carries no provenance**,
  so provenance has to be carried alongside it — a hash of the vocabulary file, stored with the model
  and compared at load time.
- Both raise rather than warn. A warning in a serving path is a line in a log nobody reads; the whole
  value here is refusing to run.
- This is the plan's Principle 13 applied to a tokenizer rather than to a checkpoint: **know what you
  loaded before you use it.**

## In production

**What a research engineer writes instead of the teaching version.** They do not write `fake_model`,
but they do draw exactly this diagram on a whiteboard about once a month, because it is the fastest way
to locate a bug. The question "at which of these eight steps is the problem?" partitions almost every
text-in/text-out failure in one move: garbled output with sensible logits is step 8; sensible output for
the wrong prompt is step 2; `IndexError` is step 4; fluent nonsense is step 2 or 8 disagreeing with each
other.

The other habit is **logging ids, not just text**, at the serving boundary. A log line that records the
first and last few ids alongside the decoded string makes tokenizer bugs visible in a way that a log of
text alone never can, because text always looks like text.

**What degrades at scale.** Step 3, batching. Real requests have different lengths, so making them
rectangular means padding, and padding introduces the plan's Silent Failure #3 — a loss that counted
positions that were never real. At small `B` the waste is invisible; at production `B` a naive padding
strategy can spend most of its compute on padding, which is why real serving stacks sort requests by
length before batching them. Day 51 builds the training version and Day 103 the serving version.

**The failure that only shows with real data.** Step 2 on input the tokenizer was not built for.
Everything upstream and downstream is length-agnostic, so a request whose text tokenizes to five times
the expected number of tokens does not fail — it silently costs five times as much, takes five times as
long, and may be truncated at the context limit with no error. The user experiences a truncated answer
and nobody's dashboard shows a fault.

**The review comment.** *"This function takes a string and returns a string, and calls the model in the
middle. Which tokenizer instance does it use, and is it the same object the training data loader used —
not just the same class?"*

**The interview question.** *"Where does the tokenizer live relative to the model?"* The weak answer is
"before it". The strong answer is: **at both ends and nowhere in between** — `encode` at the front,
`decode` at the back, and a model that only ever sees integers — and then names the consequence that
matters: the embedding table is `(V, C)` and the logits are `(B, T, V)`, so the vocabulary is baked into
two of the model's matrices, and **swapping the tokenizer without retraining changes what every row of
those matrices means** without changing a single weight or raising a single error.

**Compute tier: T0.** Two dictionaries, a list and one call to `random.Random(1337)`. The "model" here
is deliberately fake, and this part makes no claim about model quality — it is about the *interface*,
which is exactly the thing you can verify without a GPU. Day 39 runs the real forward pass through the
same eight steps.
