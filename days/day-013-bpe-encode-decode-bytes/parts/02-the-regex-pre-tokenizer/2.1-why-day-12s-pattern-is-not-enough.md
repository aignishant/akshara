# 2.1 — Why Day 12's pattern is not enough

## One-line answer

`\s*\S+|\s+` tiles the text and puts a boundary only at whitespace, so `don't`, `3.14`, `GPT-2` and
`snake_case` are each **one chunk** — and every merge learned inside them ties a letter to a digit or a
letter to punctuation, which is where a tokenizer's worst behaviour on numbers and code comes from.

---

## The story

You are sorting a big box of loose parts into a tray with compartments.

The tray you have has two compartments: one for metal things, one for everything else. It is better than
one big pile, and after an hour you notice the metal side has screws, washers, a hinge, some wire and a
watch battery all jumbled together, and finding a particular screw means going through the lot.

The dividers were not wrong. There were just not enough of them, and the ones you had were in the wrong
places for the things you actually needed to find.

---

## The idea in plain language

Day 12 part
[1.4](../../../day-012-bpe-the-merge-loop/parts/01-the-merge-loop/1.4-the-pre-tokenizer.md) introduced
the pre-tokenizer and gave it two jobs: **tile the text**, so the tokenizer stays lossless, and **define
the boundaries merges may not cross**, so tokens do not glue a word's ending to the next word's space.

`\s*\S+|\s+` does the first job perfectly. It does the second job **only at whitespace**, and that is the
gap.

Everything between two spaces is one chunk, so merges are free to run anywhere inside it. That is fine
for `hello`. It is not fine for anything that is a run of different *kinds* of character stuck together,
and English writing is full of those:

- **`don't`** — one chunk, so a merge can join `n` to `'` to `t`. The apostrophe becomes part of a word
  token rather than a thing in its own right.
- **`3.14`** — one chunk, so merges can join a digit to a full stop to a digit. Every number in the
  corpus with a decimal point trains merges that mix digits and punctuation.
- **`GPT-2`** — one chunk, so the hyphen and the digit get glued to the letters.
- **`snake_case`** — one chunk, and every identifier in a code corpus contributes merges spanning the
  underscore.
- **`  indented`** — one chunk containing two spaces *and* a word, so indentation merges into the word
  that follows it.

Each of those is a merge slot spent, and — worse — a **boundary the tokenizer can never place**. Once
`3.` is a token, the digit `3` at the start of a number is tokenized differently depending on whether a
full stop follows it.

The fix is not a different algorithm. It is **more dividers, in better places**: separate letters from
digits, isolate punctuation, cap runs of whitespace, and handle the handful of English contractions
explicitly. That is what the GPT-style pattern does and part
[2.2](2.2-the-gpt-pattern-symbol-by-symbol.md) reads it symbol by symbol.

Two things to be clear about before that.

**More boundaries cost compression.** Every boundary is a place merges are forbidden, so a
more-divided pre-tokenizer produces more tokens for the same text. Measured in part
[2.3](2.3-what-the-pattern-costs-and-buys.md): 2.51 characters per token with Day 12's pattern against
**2.39** with the GPT-style one, at the same `V = 1000`. The better pre-tokenizer is worse on the metric.

**That is the same shape as Day 12 part 1.5**, where removing the pre-tokenizer entirely improved
compression while making the tokenizer structurally worse. It is worth noticing the pattern: **on this
axis, the compression number consistently rewards the wrong choice**, and the reason to add boundaries is
consistency rather than size.

## The mechanism

The same ten strings through both patterns:

```python
import re

SIMPLE = r"\s*\S+|\s+"
GPT_LIKE = r"'(?:[sdmt]|ll|ve|re)| ?[^\W\d_]+| ?\d+| ?[^\s\w]+| ?_+|\s+(?!\S)|\s+"

CASES = ["don't", "isn't", "3.14", "1234567", "GPT-2", "snake_case",
         "hello  world", "  indented", "a\nb", "()[]{}"]

for c in CASES:
    print(f"    {ascii(c):<16} SIMPLE={[ascii(x) for x in re.findall(SIMPLE, c)]}")
    print(f"    {'':<16} GPT   ={[ascii(x) for x in re.findall(GPT_LIKE, c)]}")
```

**Line by line:**
- Both patterns are raw strings and module-level constants, per Day 12 part 1.4. `GPT_LIKE` is a
  **stdlib approximation** of the published GPT-2 pattern; part 2.2 gives the original and names every
  difference, which matters because the original needs a third-party module.
- Every case is chosen for one specific kind of boundary: an apostrophe, a decimal point, a long digit
  run, a hyphen between letters and a digit, an underscore, doubled spaces, leading indentation, a
  newline, and pure punctuation.
- `ascii()` on every chunk, per Day 12 part 2.3 — a chunk that is `' indented'` and one that is
  `'indented'` are the whole subject and look identical otherwise.
- Measured on Intel Core i3-1115G4 (2 cores / 4 threads), 11.7 GB RAM, Windows 11, CPython 3.12.12, on
  2026-08-27:

```text
    "don't"          SIMPLE=['"don\'t"']
                     GPT   =["'don'", '"\'t"']
    "isn't"          SIMPLE=['"isn\'t"']
                     GPT   =["'isn'", '"\'t"']
    '3.14'           SIMPLE=["'3.14'"]
                     GPT   =["'3'", "'.'", "'14'"]
    '1234567'        SIMPLE=["'1234567'"]
                     GPT   =["'1234567'"]
    'GPT-2'          SIMPLE=["'GPT-2'"]
                     GPT   =["'GPT'", "'-'", "'2'"]
    'snake_case'     SIMPLE=["'snake_case'"]
                     GPT   =["'snake'", "'_'", "'case'"]
    'hello  world'   SIMPLE=["'hello'", "'  world'"]
                     GPT   =["'hello'", "' '", "' world'"]
    '  indented'     SIMPLE=["'  indented'"]
                     GPT   =["' '", "' indented'"]
    'a\nb'           SIMPLE=["'a'", "'\\nb'"]
                     GPT   =["'a'", "'\\n'", "'b'"]
    '()[]{}'         SIMPLE=["'()[]{}'"]
                     GPT   =["'()[]{}'"]
```

Six of the ten rows differ, and each difference is a decision.

**`don't` becomes `don` + `'t`.** The contraction is split at the apostrophe, and `'t` is a token in its
own right — which is why the published pattern has an explicit branch for exactly `'s`, `'t`, `'re`,
`'ve`, `'m`, `'ll`, `'d`. Those seven endings are common enough in English to be worth hard-coding.

**`3.14` becomes three chunks.** Digits, then the full stop, then digits. No merge can ever join a digit
to a decimal point, so a number's tokenization no longer depends on whether it has a fractional part.
Part [2.4](2.4-the-number-that-was-three-tokens.md) shows this is necessary and not sufficient.

**`GPT-2` and `snake_case` split at the punctuation.** Letters stay with letters; the hyphen and the
underscore are their own chunks. For a code corpus that last one is significant: **every identifier
contributes merges for its parts rather than for the whole**, so `snake` and `case` are learned once
instead of once per identifier they appear in.

**`  indented` splits differently and the difference is subtle.** Day 12's pattern gives one chunk
carrying both spaces; the GPT-style one gives `' '` and `' indented'` — the *last* space attaches to the
word and the rest stand alone. That is the `\s+(?!\S)` branch, and part 2.2 explains why it is written
that way.

**`1234567` and `()[]{}` are unchanged.** Both patterns keep a run of digits together and a run of
punctuation together. That the two agree here matters: it means the difference between them is about
*mixed* runs, not about splitting everything.

Now the same thing at corpus scale:

```python
import hashlib
from pathlib import Path

raw = Path("docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md").read_bytes()
text = raw.decode("utf-8")
print("  corpus sha256[:16] :", hashlib.sha256(raw).hexdigest()[:16])

for name, pat in (("SIMPLE", SIMPLE), ("GPT_LIKE", GPT_LIKE)):
    chunks = re.findall(pat, text)
    print(f"  {name:<9} chunks={len(chunks):>6}  distinct={len(set(chunks)):>6}  "
          f"lossless={''.join(chunks) == text}")
```

**Line by line:**
- `"".join(chunks) == text` is Day 12 part 1.4's tiling assertion, applied to both. **A pattern with more
  boundaries is still required to tile**, and it would be easy to write one that does not.
- `len(set(chunks))` is what the trainer actually iterates over, and it moves in the opposite direction
  from the chunk count — more, smaller chunks means fewer *distinct* ones, because the pieces repeat more.
- Measured on this machine, 2026-08-27:

```text
  corpus sha256[:16] : 9760f2b6d4340b97
  SIMPLE    chunks= 19773  distinct=  5712  lossless=True
  GPT_LIKE  chunks= 28887  distinct=  3699  lossless=True
```

**28,887 chunks against 19,773 — 46% more — and 3,699 distinct against 5,712, which is 35% fewer.** Both
tile the text. The GPT-style pattern cuts the text into more pieces and there are fewer *kinds* of piece,
because splitting `GPT-2` into three parts means `GPT` and `2` are pieces that occur elsewhere while
`GPT-2` was unique.

That second number is the one that matters for training: part
[2.3](2.3-what-the-pattern-costs-and-buys.md) shows it makes training **faster**, which is a pleasant
side effect of a decision made for other reasons.


## When it breaks

**The loud failure — a pattern that no longer tiles.** Adding boundaries is easy to get wrong, and the
tiling assertion is what catches it:

```console
>>> pat = r"[^\W\d_]+|\d+|[^\s\w]+"          # forgot the whitespace branch
>>> chunks = re.findall(pat, "a b")
>>> chunks
['a', 'b']
>>> "".join(chunks) == "a b"
False
```

What it means: with no branch matching whitespace, the space between the words matches nothing and is
dropped. The chunk list looks entirely reasonable — two words — and the tokenizer built on it cannot
reproduce its own input, which is Day 11 part 2.1's failure returning through a "better" pattern.

**The silent failure — judging a pre-tokenizer by compression.** It is the natural thing to measure and
it points the wrong way:

```text
  SIMPLE   at V=1000 : 44,167 tokens, 2.51 chars/token
  GPT_LIKE at V=1000 : 46,311 tokens, 2.39 chars/token
  conclusion if read alone: "the simple pattern is better — 5% fewer tokens"
  what is actually true   : the simple pattern's merges span digits, apostrophes,
                            hyphens and underscores, so a word's tokenization
                            depends on what is stuck to it
  both are lossless, both round-trip, and no automatic check separates them
```

**The check that catches it** looks at the merge list for boundary crossings, exactly as Day 12 part 1.5
did, with the categories this part cares about:

```python
def audit_boundaries(merges: list[tuple[str, str]]) -> dict[str, int]:
    """Count merged tokens that span a kind boundary: letters+digits, letters+punctuation, etc."""
    def kinds(tok: str) -> set[str]:
        out = set()
        for ch in tok:
            if ch.isspace():
                out.add("space")
            elif ch.isdigit():
                out.add("digit")
            elif ch.isalpha():
                out.add("letter")
            else:
                out.add("punct")
        return out

    tokens = ["".join(m) for m in merges]
    counts = {"letter+digit": 0, "letter+punct": 0, "digit+punct": 0, "interior_space": 0}
    for t in tokens:
        k = kinds(t.lstrip(" "))
        if {"letter", "digit"} <= k:
            counts["letter+digit"] += 1
        if {"letter", "punct"} <= k:
            counts["letter+punct"] += 1
        if {"digit", "punct"} <= k:
            counts["digit+punct"] += 1
        if " " in t[1:] and t.strip():
            counts["interior_space"] += 1
    return counts
```

**Line by line:**
- `t.lstrip(" ")` removes the **leading** space before classifying, because after Day 12 part 1.4 a
  leading space is the design rather than a boundary crossing. Without it every well-behaved token would
  be flagged as `letter+space`.
- The `<=` operator on sets is subset: `{"letter", "digit"} <= k` asks whether the token contains both
  kinds. A token can be counted in more than one row, and that is correct — `a1.` spans all three.
- `interior_space` is Day 12 part 1.5's check, kept because it is the same family of question and because
  a new pattern can reintroduce it.
- The function takes **only the merge list**, so it runs against any tokenizer you are handed. Day 14 runs
  it against a production one.
- What it cannot tell you is whether a crossing is *bad*. A token `a1` is suspicious in prose and normal
  in a corpus of part numbers. **The counts are a prompt to look, not a verdict** — which is why the
  function returns a dict rather than raising.

## In production

**What a research engineer writes instead of the teaching version.** They do not design a pre-tokenizer
from scratch; they take a published pattern and adapt it, because the published ones encode a decade of
this kind of debugging. What they do write is the audit above, run against whatever they adopt, because
a pattern designed for English prose does the wrong thing on a corpus of code, chemical formulae or
tabular data — and the merge list tells you which within seconds.

The second habit is that **the pattern is versioned with the vocabulary and hashed with it**, per Day 12
part 2.1. A merge list trained under one pattern and applied under another produces chunks the merges
were never fitted to; nothing raises and compression quietly degrades.

**What degrades at scale.** The cost of a bad boundary, which compounds. Every mixed-kind merge is a slot
spent on a pattern that occurs in one context, so a vocabulary trained with too few boundaries has a long
tail of tokens like `2019` and `2020` and `_id` that are each useful in one place. At `V = 1000` that is a
few dozen slots; at `V = 50000` it can be thousands, and Day 17 measures what that does to arithmetic.

**The failure that only shows with real data.** Code and tables. Prose has few mixed-kind runs — the odd
contraction and decimal. A corpus of source code is almost entirely mixed-kind runs: identifiers with
underscores and digits, operators against names, indentation against everything. **A pre-tokenizer
validated on prose will spend most of a code corpus's vocabulary on identifier fragments**, and this
corpus, being a technical document with tables, is already a mild version of that.

**The review comment.** *"The pre-tokenizer only splits on whitespace, so `snake_case` and `3.14` are
single chunks and the merges are learning across the underscore and the decimal point. Use a pattern that
separates letters, digits and punctuation — it will cost a few percent of compression and remove a class
of inconsistency."*

**The interview question.** *"What does a pre-tokenizer actually decide?"* The weak answer is "where words
are". The strong answer names the two jobs and the trade: **it has to tile the text so the tokenizer stays
lossless, and it decides which boundaries merges may never cross — and every boundary you add costs
compression and buys consistency.** Then the measurement: *going from a whitespace-only pattern to a
GPT-style one took my corpus from 2.51 to 2.39 characters per token, so the metric got worse while
`3.14` stopped being one chunk and a number's tokenization stopped depending on its decimal point.*

**Compute tier: T0.** The standard library on a laptop, over a 113,283-byte file in this repository. No
packages, no network, no GPU, no seed. The ten-case table does not depend on the corpus at all and
reproduces everywhere; the chunk counts reproduce for anyone whose corpus hash matches
`9760f2b6d4340b97`. **`GPT_LIKE` is a stdlib approximation and part 2.2 names its differences from the
published pattern** — the comparison in this part is between two patterns that both run on
`re`, which is the honest thing to compare when one library is not installed. What T0 cannot show is
which pattern trains a better model; that is Day 17.

```python
import hashlib
import re
from pathlib import Path

SIMPLE = r"\s*\S+|\s+"
GPT_LIKE = r"'(?:[sdmt]|ll|ve|re)| ?[^\W\d_]+| ?\d+| ?[^\s\w]+| ?_+|\s+(?!\S)|\s+"

for c in ["don't", "3.14", "GPT-2", "snake_case", "  indented", "()[]{}"]:
```
