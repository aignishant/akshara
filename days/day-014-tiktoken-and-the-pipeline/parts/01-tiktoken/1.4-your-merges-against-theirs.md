# 1.4 — Your merges against theirs

## One-line answer

Trained on 113 KB of markdown, your merge list shares **8 of its first 10** merges with a list trained on a
web crawl thousands of times larger — and the agreement decays to **46.1%** by rank 744, with the 167
merges that appear in yours and *never* in `gpt2`'s 50,000 being almost entirely markdown: `Ġ---`, `.**`,
`ĠĠĠĠ`.

---

## The story

Two people keep a shopping list on the fridge for a month. One lives alone in a flat; the other cooks for
four in a house across the country. They have never met.

Compare the lists and the top of each is nearly the same: milk, bread, eggs, onions. Not because they agree
about anything — because that is what running a kitchen costs.

Go further down and the lists separate completely. One has cat food. One has school snacks. By the bottom
of the page you could tell, without being told, which household is which.

The top of the list is the language. The bottom is the person.

---

## The idea in plain language

Day 12 part
[2.5](../../../day-012-bpe-the-merge-loop/parts/02-training-a-vocabulary/2.5-the-vocabulary-that-depended-on-the-order-of-the-corpus.md)
showed that a merge list is a fingerprint of its corpus. That was demonstrated by *changing* the corpus and
watching the list change. This part does the opposite experiment: compare two lists trained on corpora that
have nothing to do with each other, and see what survives.

The result has a shape, and the shape is the lesson:

- **The first few merges are almost universal.** ` t`, ` a`, `he`, `in`, `re`, `on` — these are the most
  common letter pairs in English. Any English corpus of any size produces roughly these, because they are
  facts about the language, not about the text.
- **The middle is common vocabulary.** `ing`, `ion`, `the`, ` and` — still English, less universal.
- **The tail is the corpus.** `Ġ---` is a markdown horizontal rule. `.**` is a full stop before bold text.
  `ĠĠĠĠ` is indentation. None of these are English; all of them are *this file*.

So a merge list is stratified. And since a vocabulary is fixed at training time and used forever, **the
tail is the part you are permanently paying for**, and the part that only matches text like your training
text.

---

## Why Akshara needs it

**Because it tells you what a small corpus actually costs.** The temptation, after part
[1.1](1.1-the-same-corpus-through-both.md), is to conclude the 113 KB corpus is fine — after all,
compression at equal `V` matched a production library to within 0.04%. That comparison used the *same*
corpus for both. This part is the other question: how good is the *vocabulary* itself, on text that is not
this file? The 167 markdown-only merges are 22.4% of your table spent on a formatting language.

**Because you are going to have to choose a training corpus for Akshara's real tokenizer**, and Day 17
(`TOK-20`) makes that choice explicitly. This part gives you the tool: look at the merge list and read off
what it was trained on.

**Because it is a decontamination skill.** Silent Failure #1 is contamination — the eval set was in the
training corpus. A merge list that contains a token for a distinctive string from your benchmark is
evidence about what the corpus contained, and it is evidence that survives even when the corpus itself is
not available for inspection. Day 23 (`TRAIN-23`) builds the real check; this is the intuition it rests on.

## The mechanism

### Step 1 — load both lists

```python
import json
from pathlib import Path
from tiktoken.load import read_file_cached

vb = read_file_cached(
    "https://openaipublic.blob.core.windows.net/gpt-2/encodings/main/vocab.bpe",
    expected_hash="1ce1664773c50f3e0cc8842619a93edc4624525b728b188a9e0be33b7726adc5",
)
gpt2 = [tuple(line.split()) for line in vb.decode().splitlines()[1:]]
mine = [tuple(m) for m in json.loads(
    Path("day13_merges_1000.json").read_text(encoding="utf-8"))["merges"]]

print("gpt2 merges:", len(gpt2), " mine:", len(mine))
```

**Line by line:**

- `.splitlines()[1:]` skips `#version: 0.2`, the off-by-one from part
  [1.3](1.3-what-a-vocabulary-file-looks-like.md). Getting this wrong shifts every rank by one and makes
  the whole comparison meaningless while still producing plausible-looking output.
- `tuple(line.split())` — the pairs are space-separated, and neither element can contain a space, because
  the byte→unicode map from Day 13 part 3.2 guarantees every symbol is printable and non-space. That
  guarantee is exactly what makes this file format possible; it was not an aesthetic choice.
- `tuple` rather than `list` so the pairs are hashable and can go into a `set` below.
- Both lists use the *same* byte→unicode convention, which is the only reason they are comparable at all.
  If yours had used raw characters, `Ġ` would mean nothing and every space-initial merge would look like a
  disagreement.

### Step 2 — the top of the lists

```python
print("rank  GPT-2          mine")
for i in range(10):
    print(f"{i:4d}  {str(gpt2[i]):14s} {mine[i]}")
```

**Line by line:**

- Printing the two lists **side by side at the same index** is the whole method. Printing them one after
  the other would show you two plausible lists of English letter pairs and no relationship between them.
- `str(gpt2[i]):14s` — a tuple has no format spec of its own, so it is converted to a string first and
  then padded. Without the `str()` the `:14s` raises `TypeError: unsupported format string passed to
  tuple.__format__`.
- `range(10)` and not more: the top of the list is where the corpora are most alike, and the point of this
  step is to establish that before the decay in step 3 undercuts it.

Measured, corpus `9760f2b6d4340b97`:

```text
rank  GPT-2          mine
   0  ('Ġ', 't')     ('Ġ', '|')
   1  ('Ġ', 'a')     ('Ġ', 'a')
   2  ('h', 'e')     ('Ġ', 't')
   3  ('i', 'n')     ('i', 'n')
   4  ('r', 'e')     ('h', 'e')
   5  ('o', 'n')     ('o', 'n')
   6  ('Ġt', 'he')   ('e', 'r')
   7  ('e', 'r')     ('a', 't')
   8  ('Ġ', 's')     ('r', 'e')
   9  ('a', 't')     ('e', 'n')
```

Eight of the first ten merges appear in both lists. They are in different *positions* — mine puts `Ġt`
third where `gpt2` puts it first — but the same pairs are being chosen.

The single most visible difference is mine at rank 0: `('Ġ', '|')`, a space followed by a pipe. That is a
markdown table. `gpt2`'s corpus has almost no tables; this plan is full of them, and the very first thing
the trainer noticed was the table syntax. **Rank 0 is the most common pair in the corpus, so rank 0 is the
loudest single statement about what the corpus is.**

### Step 3 — how fast does the agreement decay?

```python
for k in (10, 50, 100, 300, 744):
    shared = len(set(gpt2[:k]) & set(mine[:k]))
    print(f"first {k:4d}: {shared:4d} shared = {shared / k * 100:5.1f}%")
```

**Line by line:**

- `set(gpt2[:k]) & set(mine[:k])` compares the first `k` of each as *sets*, so a merge counts as shared if
  both lists contain it anywhere in that window. This is the right question here — "did both trainers think
  this pair was worth a slot?" — and it is deliberately weaker than the position-by-position comparison in
  part [2.2](../02-the-tokenizers-pipeline/2.2-the-same-vocabulary-trained-twice.md), which asks a
  different question about tie-breaking between two implementations on the *same* corpus.

Measured:

| window | shared | agreement |
| ---: | ---: | ---: |
| first 10 | 8 | **80.0%** |
| first 50 | 36 | 72.0% |
| first 100 | 68 | 68.0% |
| first 300 | 169 | 56.3% |
| first 744 | 343 | **46.1%** |

A clean monotone decay from 80% to 46%. The top of the list is shared; the bottom is not.

Widening the window changes the picture again:

```python
anywhere = len(set(mine) & set(gpt2))
print(f"mine, anywhere in GPT-2's 50,000: {anywhere} = {anywhere / len(mine) * 100:.1f}%")
```

**Line by line:**

- `set(gpt2)` with **no slice** — the whole 50,000, not a matching window. That is the difference from
  step 3, and it changes the question from "did both trainers reach this pair at the same point?" to "did
  the larger trainer ever want this pair at all?"
- Dividing by `len(mine)` and not by `len(gpt2)` fixes the direction of the comparison. The other
  denominator gives 1.2% and answers a different question — see **When it breaks**.

```text
mine, anywhere in GPT-2's 50,000: 577 = 77.6%
```

So of your 744 merges, 577 are pairs `gpt2` also learned — just later, further down its much longer list.
Those are ordinary English that a small corpus reaches early and a large one gets to eventually.

### Step 4 — the 167 that are yours alone

```python
gpt2_set = set(gpt2)
missing = [m for m in mine if m not in gpt2_set]
print(f"in mine, never in GPT-2's 50,000: {len(missing)}")
print(missing[:12])
```

```text
in mine, never in GPT-2's 50,000: 167
[('Ġ', 'Ġ'), ('Ġâ', 'Ģ'), ('ĠĠ', 'ĠĠ'), ('Ġ', '---'), ('Ġt', 'hat'),
 ('Ċ', 'ĠĠ'), ('Ġw', 'hat'), ('.', '**'), ('ra', 'in'), ('R', 'CH'),
 ('iv', 'e'), ('Ġ', 'or')]
```

**Line by line:**

- `set(gpt2)` once, outside the comprehension. Building it inside would rebuild a 50,000-element set on
  every one of the 744 iterations.

Read the list with the byte→unicode map in hand (Day 13 part 3.2 — `Ġ` is a space, `Ċ` is a newline):

| Merge | Reads as | What it is |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `('Ġ', 'Ġ')` | two spaces | indentation |
| `('ĠĠ', 'ĠĠ')` | four spaces | more indentation |
| `('Ċ', 'ĠĠ')` | newline + two spaces | a wrapped, indented line |
| `('Ġ', '---')` | ` ---` | a markdown table rule |
| `('.', '**')` | `.**` | a full stop ending bold text |
| `('Ġâ', 'Ģ')` | part of `—` | the em dash, mid-reassembly |

Six of the twelve are **markdown and whitespace**, not English. And `('Ġâ', 'Ģ')` is the partial em dash
from Day 13 part
[3.4](../../../day-013-bpe-encode-decode-bytes/parts/03-byte-level-bpe/3.4-the-vocabulary-you-cannot-read.md)
— a merge that is not a token for anything, just two thirds of one character.

167 of 744 is **22.4% of your merge table** spent on things that are not the English language. `gpt2` spent
none of its first 50,000 on them.

## Shapes

| Name | Symbolic | Concrete | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `gpt2` | `(50000, 2)` | `(50000, 2)` | ordered pairs; rank is the row index |
| `mine` | `(744, 2)` | `(744, 2)` | same convention, 67× shorter |
| `set(mine) & set(gpt2)` | `(n_shared,)` | `(577,)` | pairs both trainers chose |
| `missing` | `(n_unique,)` | `(167,)` | pairs only this corpus produced |

`577 + 167 = 744`. ✅

## When it breaks

**The comparison is only meaningful if both lists use the same byte convention.** Forget the `[1:]` slice
and the version line becomes a merge, shifting every rank:

```text
rank  GPT-2                   mine
   0  ('#version:', '0.2')    ('Ġ', '|')
   1  ('Ġ', 't')              ('Ġ', 'a')
```

Now the top-10 agreement reads 7/10 instead of 8/10 and every conclusion is quietly slightly wrong. Nothing
raises. This is why part 1.3 flagged the version line twice.

**A high overlap does not mean a good vocabulary.** 77.6% of your merges appear somewhere in `gpt2`'s
table. That sounds like a pass. It is not a measurement of quality — it is a measurement of *English*.
Compression on held-out text is the measurement of quality, and part
[3.2](../03-the-remaining-gap/3.2-the-tax-a-vocabulary-charges-a-language.md) does it.

**The direction of the comparison matters and is easy to get backwards.** "577 of my 744 are in `gpt2`" is
77.6%. The reverse — "577 of `gpt2`'s 50,000 are in mine" — is 1.2%, and means something entirely
different. Both are true. Only one answers "is my small vocabulary made of reasonable English pieces?"

## In production

**This is how you audit an inherited tokenizer.** You are handed a `tokenizer.json` with no documentation.
Sort the merges, read the tail, and you will know within minutes whether it was trained on code, on web
text, on one language or many, and often on which. The tail is the corpus, and the tail is readable.

**It is also how vocabulary contamination gets found.** If a merge list contains a token for a distinctive
string from your benchmark — an unusual proper noun, a specific formatting artifact — that string was
frequent in the tokenizer's training corpus. It does not prove the eval set was in the *model's* training
data, since the two corpora can differ, but in practice they usually overlap, and it is cheap evidence
available without access to either corpus.

**Domain vocabularies are a real technique with a real cost.** Training a tokenizer on code, or on a single
non-Latin language, buys substantial compression on that domain — part 3.2 measures how much a
general-purpose vocabulary charges Hindi. The cost is that the model must be trained from scratch with it,
because changing a vocabulary invalidates every embedding. This is the trade Day 17 (`TOK-20`) makes you
work through.

**Nobody trains a production vocabulary on 113 KB, and the 22.4% figure is why.** At this size a
substantial slice of the table goes to your corpus's formatting rather than its language. The fix is more
and more varied text, not a bigger `V` — a bigger `V` on the same corpus just buys more markdown.

## Check yourself

```python
import json
from pathlib import Path
from tiktoken.load import read_file_cached

vb = read_file_cached(
    "https://openaipublic.blob.core.windows.net/gpt-2/encodings/main/vocab.bpe",
    expected_hash="1ce1664773c50f3e0cc8842619a93edc4624525b728b188a9e0be33b7726adc5")
gpt2 = [tuple(l.split()) for l in vb.decode().splitlines()[1:]]
mine = [tuple(m) for m in json.loads(
    Path("day13_merges_1000.json").read_text(encoding="utf-8"))["merges"]]

# the eval that can go red: agreement must DECAY, not wander
windows = [10, 50, 100, 300, 744]
agree = [len(set(gpt2[:k]) & set(mine[:k])) / k for k in windows]
for k, a in zip(windows, agree):
    print(f"first {k:4d}: {a * 100:5.1f}%")
assert agree == sorted(agree, reverse=True), \
    "agreement is not monotonically decaying - the stratification claim is wrong for your corpus"

gpt2_set = set(gpt2)
missing = [m for m in mine if m not in gpt2_set]
print(f"\nyours alone: {len(missing)} = {len(missing) / len(mine) * 100:.1f}% of your table")

# decode them back to readable text and look
from bpe13 import BYTE_ENCODER          # your Day 13 module

BYTE_DECODER = {v: k for k, v in BYTE_ENCODER.items()}

def readable(sym):
    try:
        return bytes(BYTE_DECODER[c] for c in sym).decode("utf-8")
    except (KeyError, UnicodeDecodeError):
        return f"<incomplete: {sym}>"

for pair in missing[:15]:
    print(f"  {str(pair):22s} -> {readable(pair[0] + pair[1])!r}")
```

**Line by line:**

- The `assert` is the eval that can go red, and it tests the *claim* rather than the numbers. The claim is
  "agreement decays with rank". If your corpus produces a non-monotone curve, the stratification story does
  not hold for you and you should find out why rather than copying my table.
- `readable` reverses the byte→unicode map to show what each merge actually *is*. The `except
  UnicodeDecodeError` is not defensive padding — it is the expected path for merges like `('Ġâ', 'Ģ')`
  that are two thirds of a character, and labelling them `<incomplete>` is the honest output. Day 13 part
  3.4 measured 16 such merges in this list.
