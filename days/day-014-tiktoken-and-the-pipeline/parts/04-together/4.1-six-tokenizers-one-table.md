# 4.1 — Six tokenizers, one table

## One-line answer

Four days of building and one day of comparing produce six tokenizers on one corpus, and the table says
something uncomfortable: the **word** tokenizer still wins on compression at a tenth of `gpt2`'s
vocabulary, and it is still the one you cannot use — because compression was never the property that
mattered.

---

## The story

You have been keeping a note of what six different shops charge for the same bag of rice. Today you finally
write the six prices in one column.

The cheapest one is the shop that sometimes has no rice.

That is not a reason to strike it off the list — it is a reason to add a second column, because "price"
was never the whole question and you have been carrying the other half of it in your head this whole time.
Writing it down is what turns six numbers into a decision.

---

## The idea in plain language

Day 11 part [3.1](../../../day-011-character-and-word-tokenizers/parts/03-together/3.1-two-tokenizers-one-table.md)
put two tokenizers in a table. Day 12 made it three, Day 13 four. Today it becomes six, and the two new
rows are the ones that were supposed to settle the matter.

They do not settle it. What they do is make a different point visible: **every column in this table is a
different question, and the tokenizer that wins depends entirely on which column you read.** A day spent
comparing is wasted unless it ends with the comparison written down in a form that shows the trade, and
that is what this part is.

---

## Why Akshara needs it

**Because Day 16 chooses one of these and lives with it.** The chat template, the training run, the serving
path and every embedding in the model are downstream of that choice. It should be made from a table, not
from the last thing you built.

**Because the ledger is the memory.** 162 days is long enough that Day 30 forgets Day 11 — the plan says so
in as many words. This table is the artifact that survives, and every number in it is measured on one
corpus with a known hash, so the comparison is real rather than remembered.

**Because Day 17 needs a baseline.** The failure lab (`TOK-18`, `TOK-19`, `TOK-20`) breaks tokenizers
deliberately and asks what it costs. You cannot measure a cost without this table.

---

## The mechanism

All six on `docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md`, sha256 `9760f2b6d4340b97`, 113,283 bytes, 110,837 code points.

```python
import hashlib
from pathlib import Path

P = Path("docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md")
text, raw = P.read_text(encoding="utf-8"), P.read_bytes()
print("corpus", hashlib.sha256(raw).hexdigest()[:16], len(raw), "bytes", len(text), "chars")
```

**Line by line:**

- One `text` variable, read once, used by all six. Day 13 part
  [4.1](../../../day-013-bpe-encode-decode-bytes/parts/04-together/4.1-four-tokenizers-one-table.md) made
  this rule and it is the rule that makes the table a comparison rather than six separate facts.
- The hash is printed first, every time. If yours differs, every number below is yours and not mine.

### The table

| # | Tokenizer | `V` | tokens | chars/token | chars in a 1,024 window | round trip | out-of-vocabulary |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | --- | --- |
| 1 | character (D11) | 151 | 110,837 | 1.000 | 1,024 | exact | **none, by construction** |
| 2 | word (D11) | 3,118 | 29,081 | **3.811** | **3,903** | **lossy** | **UNK or drop** |
| 3 | char BPE (D12) | 1,000 | 42,130 | 2.631 | 2,694 | exact *on this corpus* | fails on an unseen character |
| 4 | byte BPE (D13) | 1,000 | 46,311 | 2.393 | 2,451 | exact | **zero, by construction** |
| 5 | `tokenizers` (D14) | 1,000 | 46,291 | 2.394 | 2,452 | exact | **zero, by construction** |
| 6 | `tiktoken` `gpt2` (D14) | 50,257 | 33,405 | 3.318 | 3,398 | exact | **zero, by construction** |

Row 2's word rule is `\w+|[^\w\s]` — one token per word or punctuation mark. Day 11 used a different rule,
so your Day 11 numbers will differ; the rule is stated here so the row is reproducible rather than
comparable-by-accident.

### Reading it

**Row 2 wins the compression column and cannot be used.** 3.811 chars/token beats `gpt2`'s 3.318, with a
vocabulary 16× smaller. And it is lossy — Day 11 part
[2.3](../../../day-011-character-and-word-tokenizers/parts/02-word-level/2.3-the-out-of-vocabulary-wall.md)
measured 10.41% of word occurrences falling outside an uncapped vocabulary, and every one of those becomes
`UNK` or vanishes. **Compression is not the objective function.** It is one column, and the "round trip"
column has a veto.

That is the single most useful thing in this table, and it is only visible because the losing rows were
kept.

**Rows 4 and 5 are the same tokenizer, written twice.** 46,311 versus 46,291 — 0.043% apart, one in Python
and one in Rust, diverging only where counts tie (part
[2.2](../02-the-tokenizers-pipeline/2.2-the-same-vocabulary-trained-twice.md)). This is the row that says
your four days of building produced something correct.

**Row 4 is worse than row 3, and that is the right trade.** Byte-level BPE compresses less than
character-level BPE at the same `V` — 2.393 against 2.631 — because 256 byte symbols crowd out merges that
character-level spends on the 151 characters it actually saw. What it buys is the difference between
"exact *on this corpus*" and "exact". Day 13 part
[3.1](../../../day-013-bpe-encode-decode-bytes/parts/03-byte-level-bpe/3.1-the-alphabet-becomes-256.md)
made that trade knowingly; the table is where it is priced.

**Row 6 beats row 4 by 39% and it is not the code.** 50× the vocabulary and a corpus thousands of times
larger. At equal `V` the gap closes to 0.043%.

### The column the table does not have

Every row above is measured on English markdown. Part
[3.2](../03-the-remaining-gap/3.2-the-tax-a-vocabulary-charges-a-language.md) adds the column that changes
the ranking:

| Tokenizer | English | Hindi | Japanese |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| byte BPE `V=1000` (D13) | 2.19 chars/tok | **0.38** | 0.33 |
| `gpt2` | 4.38 | 0.64 | 0.69 |
| `cl100k_base` | 4.38 | 0.97 | 0.95 |
| `o200k_base` | 4.38 | **3.50** | 1.20 |

Rows 4, 5 and 6 all score "zero out-of-vocabulary" in the main table and all three charge Hindi several
times what they charge English. **A guarantee about possibility is not a statement about cost**, and no
column in the first table would have told you.

---

## Shapes

| Name | Symbolic | Range across the six | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `V` | scalar | 151 → 50,257 | embedding rows; `(V, C)` parameters |
| `T` for this corpus | `(T,)` | 29,081 → 110,837 | **3.8× spread** for the same text |
| chars per 1,024 window | scalar | 1,024 → 3,903 | how much text the model can see |
| attention work, `O(T²)` | scalar | 1× → **14.5×** | row 1 against row 2, same document |

The last row is why row 1 lost. A character tokenizer is exact, has no unknowns and is trivially simple —
and costs 14.5× the attention work of the word tokenizer on the same document, which Day 11 part
[1.3](../../../day-011-character-and-word-tokenizers/parts/01-character-level/1.3-the-sequence-length-problem.md)
measured as the reason it is unusable at scale.

---

## When it breaks

**Ranking on one column.** The table is built so this is hard to do accidentally, and it is exactly what a
single "compression benchmark" invites. Row 2 wins compression and loses everything else.

**Comparing rows measured on different corpora.** Every number here comes from one file with a printed
hash. Day 9's figures no longer reproduce against the current plan, which is why the hash is printed in
every script this day — a table whose rows come from different corpus versions is not a table, it is a
coincidence.

**Treating "zero OOV" as a pass mark.** Rows 4, 5 and 6 all have it. They differ by 39% on compression and
by 5.5× on Hindi. The guarantee is a floor, not a score.

**Reading row 5 as validating row 4's *design*.** It validates the *implementation*. Both make the same
choices about pre-tokenizer and byte mapping, so they agree; that agreement says nothing about whether
those choices were good, which is what rows 1, 2, 3 and 6 are for.

---

## In production

**This table is the artifact, and it should be regenerated, not remembered.** A script that rebuilds it
from the corpus, prints the hash and fails on a regression is worth more than the numbers written down
once. When a tokenizer changes, the table changes, and the diff is the review.

**The real choice adds columns this corpus cannot fill.** Multilingual rate (part 3.2 begins it), code,
numbers, whitespace, and the model-quality question none of these columns touch: does a model trained with
this vocabulary actually do better? That last one costs a training run to answer, which is why the
proxy columns matter so much and why they are chosen carefully.

**`V` is a parameter budget, not a free dial.** At `C = 768`, row 6's 50,257 vocabulary is 38.6M embedding
parameters plus the same again for the output projection. For a small model that can exceed everything
else combined, which is the arithmetic Day 26 (`EFF-01`) makes you do properly.

**Nobody trains a vocabulary on 113 KB.** Part [1.4](../01-tiktoken/1.4-your-merges-against-theirs.md)
measured 22.4% of your merge table going to markdown formatting rather than language. Every row in this
table shares that limitation, which means the table compares implementations honestly and does not tell
you what any of them would do on a real corpus.

```python
text, raw = P.read_text(encoding="utf-8"), P.read_bytes()
print("corpus", hashlib.sha256(raw).hexdigest()[:16])

rows = []           # (name, V, n_tokens, lossless)
# ... fill from your own Day 11, 12, 13 and today's code ...

print(f"{'tokenizer':26s} {'V':>7s} {'tokens':>8s} {'chars/tok':>10s} {'1024-win':>9s} lossless")
for name, V, n, lossless in rows:
    print(f"{name:26s} {V:7d} {n:8d} {len(text)/n:10.3f} {1024*len(text)/n:9.0f} {lossless}")

# the eval that can go red: the best compressor must NOT be the recommendation
best = max(rows, key=lambda r: len(text) / r[2])
assert not best[3] or best[1] > 10000, \
    f"{best[0]} wins compression - check whether it is lossless before recommending it"

# every lossless tokenizer must actually round-trip, on non-English too
PROBES = ["The plan.", "café ½", "योजना", "def f(x): return x ** 2"]
for name, V, n, lossless, encode, decode in detailed_rows:
    if not lossless:
        continue
    for p in PROBES:
        assert decode(encode(p)) == p, f"{name} claims lossless but failed on {p!r}"
print("every lossless row round-trips all four probes")
```

**Line by line:**

- `rows` is left for you to fill, because the point of the exercise is assembling your own six numbers from
  four days of your own code. Copying my table defeats it, and Principle 8 says the numbers must be yours.
- The first `assert` is the eval that can go red and it encodes this part's argument as a test: if the
  best-compressing row is lossy, say so out loud rather than recommending it. It is an unusual assertion —
  a check on your *conclusion*, not on your data.
- `PROBES` deliberately includes an accented character, a fraction, Devanagari and code. A lossless claim
  tested only on `"The plan."` is Silent Failure #5, and rows 1, 3, 4, 5 and 6 all pass an English-only
  probe while only 4, 5 and 6 survive `"योजना"`.
