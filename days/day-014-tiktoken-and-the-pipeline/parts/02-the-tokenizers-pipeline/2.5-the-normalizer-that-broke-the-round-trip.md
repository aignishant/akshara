# 2.5 — 💥 The normalizer that broke the round trip

## One-line answer

Switch on an `NFKC` normalizer and `decode(encode(s)) == s` becomes **False** — `½` comes back as `1⁄2` —
and the offsets stop tiling: four consecutive tokens all report the same one-character span `(14, 15)`,
because the library is honestly answering an unanswerable question.

---

## The story

🎬 **The scene.** A form asks for a phone number and helpfully cleans up what people type — strips spaces,
brackets and dashes so `(020) 7946 0018` becomes `02079460018`. It works beautifully for two years.

😬 **The naive fix — there isn't one yet, because nothing looks broken.** Then somebody asks for the
original as typed, for a receipt. And the original is gone. The form never kept it; it kept the cleaned
version and threw the typed one away at the moment of typing.

💥 **Why it fails.** Cleaning was not a *view* of the data. It was an *edit*, applied before anything was
stored, and edits are not reversible unless you keep the original beside them. The system was correct at
every step and has still permanently lost information nobody decided to discard.

💡 **The insight.** There are two kinds of transformation: the ones you can undo and the ones you cannot.
Both look identical while everything is going well. The difference only appears when somebody asks a
question that requires going backwards — and by then it is far too late to start keeping the original.

---

## The idea in plain language

Part [2.1](2.1-five-stages-not-one.md) said stage 1, the normalizer, is the only stage that **changes the
text**, and flagged that the round trip survives only while stage 1 is lossless. This part switches it on
and watches both promises break at once.

Day 10 part
[1.3](../../../day-010-unicode-code-points-bytes/parts/01-code-points-and-graphemes/1.3-normalization-one-spelling-for-the-same-thing.md)
introduced the four normalization forms. The critical division is:

| Form | What it does | Reversible? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **NFC** / **NFD** | *canonical* — reorders and composes; `é` as one code point or two | **yes**, they round-trip to each other |
| **NFKC** / **NFKD** | *compatibility* — replaces characters with what they "mean" | **no**, permanently |

The `K` is for compatibility, and it is a one-way door. `ﬁ` (a single ligature character) becomes `fi`
(two letters). `½` becomes `1⁄2`. `²` becomes `2`. These are judgements about meaning, not encoding, and
nothing records what was there before.

A tokenizer that applies NFKC is therefore **editing your corpus** before it ever sees a merge. Every
document is silently rewritten. That may be exactly what you want — or it may destroy the distinction your
task depends on.

---

## Why Akshara needs it

**Because the choice is made once and lives forever.** The normalizer is part of the tokenizer file, and
the model is trained on whatever comes out of it. If you train with NFKC and serve without it, or the
reverse, you have Silent Failure #2 — and unlike the pre-tokenizer mismatch in part
[3.3](../03-the-remaining-gap/3.3-the-mismatch-the-library-will-not-catch.md), this one also changes the
*text*, so it is even harder to spot by comparing ids on ASCII.

**Because the round-trip test has been the safety net for three days**, and this is where it fails. Day 13
part [1.2](../../../day-013-bpe-encode-decode-bytes/parts/01-encode-and-decode/1.2-decode-is-a-lookup-not-a-search.md)
established `encode(decode(y)) != y`. This part establishes the other direction: `decode(encode(s)) != s`.
Both directions of the round trip can fail, for different reasons, and knowing which is which is the
difference between debugging and guessing.

**Because it is the mirror of part [2.3](2.3-offsets-are-the-product.md).** Offsets were the capability the
library has and you do not. This is the price: keeping offsets accurate *through* a transformation that
changes lengths is genuinely impossible, and watching the library fail honestly at it teaches more than
watching it succeed.

---

## The mechanism

### Step 1 — a probe with two compatibility characters

```python
s = "The ﬁle costs ½ a dollar."
print(repr(s), "len =", len(s))
```

```text
'The ﬁle costs ½ a dollar.' len = 25
```

**Line by line:**

- `ﬁ` is U+FB01 LATIN SMALL LIGATURE FI — one code point that *looks* like two letters. It appears in
  text copied out of typeset documents and PDFs, which is a large fraction of real-world corpora.
- `½` is U+00BD VULGAR FRACTION ONE HALF — one code point meaning "a half".
- Both are shown as literals here so the probe reads as text. In your own file prefer the escapes —
  `"The \uFB01le costs \u00BD a dollar."` — because `ﬁ` and `fi` are visually near-identical in most
  editors, and a probe you cannot distinguish from its own normalized form is a probe that proves nothing.
  Day 10's habit, and this is the part that shows why it matters.
- 25 characters. Hold on to that number.

### Step 2 — without a normalizer, everything works

```python
plain = build(norm=None)
out = plain.encode(s)
print("tokens:", out.tokens)
print("round trip:", plain.decode(out.ids) == s)
```

**Line by line:**

- `build(norm=None)` is the part 2.1 tokenizer with stage 1 explicitly absent. Passing `None` by name
  rather than omitting the argument makes the *choice* visible in the code, which matters when the next
  block changes exactly that one thing.
- Printing `tokens` alongside the round-trip result shows both what it cost and that it was correct. The
  cost is ugly and the correctness is total, and holding both in view is the setup for step 3.

```text
tokens: ['The', 'Ġ', 'ï', '¬', 'ģ', 'le', 'Ġc', 'ost', 's', 'ĠÂ', '½', 'Ġa', 'Ġd', 'oll', 'ar', '.']
round trip: True
```

The ligature became three tokens — `ï`, `¬`, `ģ` — because U+FB01 is three bytes in UTF-8 and this
vocabulary learned no merge for them. Ugly, expensive, and **completely correct**. Day 13 part
[3.3](../../../day-013-bpe-encode-decode-bytes/parts/03-byte-level-bpe/3.3-zero-out-of-vocabulary.md)'s
guarantee holds: no unknown tokens, exact round trip, at the cost of one token per byte.

### Step 3 — switch on NFKC

```python
from tokenizers import normalizers

nfkc = build(norm=normalizers.NFKC())
print("normalized:", repr(nfkc.normalizer.normalize_str(s)),
      "len =", len(nfkc.normalizer.normalize_str(s)))
out = nfkc.encode(s)
print("tokens    :", out.tokens)
print("decode    :", repr(nfkc.decode(out.ids)))
print("round trip:", nfkc.decode(out.ids) == s)
```

**Line by line:**

- `normalizers.NFKC()` is stage 1. Everything else about the tokenizer is unchanged, including the
  training call — so the merge list is trained on normalized text too, which is correct and is also why the
  token counts differ from step 2.
- `normalize_str(s)` runs stage 1 alone, so you can see the edit before the tokenizer acts on it. Always
  print this when debugging a normalizer; it is the only place the change is visible as text.

Measured, `tokenizers==0.23.1`:

```text
normalized: 'The file costs 1⁄2 a dollar.' len = 28
tokens    : ['The', 'Ġfil', 'e', 'Ġc', 'ost', 's', 'Ġ1', 'â', 'ģ', 'Ħ', '2', 'Ġa', 'Ġd', 'oll', 'ar', '.']
decode    : 'The file costs 1⁄2 a dollar.'
round trip: False
```

**Three things happened, and only one of them is obvious.**

**The string got longer.** 25 characters in, 28 out. `ﬁ` → `fi` adds one; `½` → `1⁄2` adds two.

**The round trip is now False.** `½` went in and `1⁄2` came out. Not an error, not a warning — a different
string, returned confidently. Everything that has been checking `decode(encode(s)) == s` for three days
would fail here, and everything that *has not been checking* would ship it.

**The ligature got cheaper and the fraction got dearer, by exactly the same amount.** Encoding the two
words on their own:

| word | no normalizer | NFKC |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `' ﬁle'` | `['Ġ','ï','¬','ģ','le']` — **5 tokens** | `['Ġfil','e']` — **2 tokens** |
| `' ½'` | `['ĠÂ','½']` — **2 tokens** | `['Ġ1','â','ģ','Ħ','2']` — **5 tokens** |

`ﬁ` normalized into ordinary letters the merge list already knows, so it collapsed 5 → 2. `½` normalized
into `1⁄2`, where U+2044 FRACTION SLASH is a three-byte character the vocabulary has never seen, so it
expanded 2 → 5. **NFKC did not make text cheaper; it moved the cost.** Which direction it moves for *your*
corpus is an empirical question, and the answer is not knowable from the fact that normalization sounds
like simplification.

### Step 4 — the offsets tell the truth, and the truth is not usable

```python
for token, (a, b) in zip(out.tokens, out.offsets):
    print(f"   {token!r:6s} ({a},{b}) -> {s[a:b]!r}")
```

**Line by line:**

- `s[a:b]` slices the **original** string, not the normalized one. That is what makes the collapse visible:
  had this sliced the normalized text, every row would look sensible and the information loss would be
  hidden by the very code meant to expose it.
- Identical to part 2.3's loop, deliberately — the same probe code against a different tokenizer, so any
  difference in the output is attributable to the normalizer alone.

```text
   'The'  (0,3) -> 'The'
   'Ġfil' (3,6) -> ' ﬁl'
   'e'    (6,7) -> 'e'
   'Ġc'   (7,9) -> ' c'
   'ost'  (9,12) -> 'ost'
   's'    (12,13) -> 's'
   'Ġ1'   (13,15) -> ' ½'
   'â'    (14,15) -> '½'
   'ģ'    (14,15) -> '½'
   'Ħ'    (14,15) -> '½'
   '2'    (14,15) -> '½'
   'Ġa'   (15,17) -> ' a'
   ...
   '.'    (24,25) -> '.'
```

Look at `'Ġfil'` → `' ﬁl'`. The token is four characters and the span is three, because the ligature it
came from is one character that became two. And look at the four rows claiming `(14, 15)`: tokens `â`,
`ģ`, `Ħ` and `2` **all report the same single source character**, `½`.

**They are not wrong.** Ask "which characters of the original did the token `2` come from?" and the only
true answer is "the `½`, along with three other tokens". The library is being honest about a question that
has no clean answer. But the consequence is concrete: offsets no longer partition the input, so any
highlighter, span extractor or citation builder that assumed they did is now broken.

Measured against part 2.3's invariants, on this probe:

| Invariant | No normalizer | NFKC |
| --- | --- | --- |
| offsets parallel to ids | ✅ | ✅ |
| first starts at 0 | ✅ | ✅ |
| last ends at `len(s)` | ✅ | ✅ |
| no gaps or overlaps | ❌ | ❌ |
| distinct spans | 14 / 16 | **13 / 16** |
| **`decode(encode(s)) == s`** | ✅ | ❌ |

Two things to read here, and the first is a correction to a tempting assumption.

**Tiling already fails without the normalizer.** This probe contains multi-byte characters, and part
[2.3](2.3-offsets-are-the-product.md) established that a multi-byte character split across tokens makes
those tokens share a span — `café` did exactly this. So the normalizer is not what *introduces* overlap;
it makes it worse, from 2 collapsed spans to 3, and the worst single span goes from 2 tokens to 4.

**The round trip, by contrast, breaks only under NFKC.** That is the clean signal in this table, and it is
the one that separates a lossless stage from a lossy one. The endpoints survive in both cases, because the
library keeps offsets in the original string's coordinate system.

---

## Shapes

| Name | Symbolic | No normalizer | NFKC | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `s` | `(n_chars,)` | `(25,)` | `(25,)` | what the caller passed |
| after stage 1 | `(n_chars',)` | `(25,)` | **`(28,)`** | what the tokenizer actually sees |
| `ids` | `(T,)` | `(16,)` | `(16,)` | coincidentally equal; not a rule |
| `offsets` | `(T, 2)` | `(16, 2)` | `(16, 2)` | still parallel; neither is a partition |
| distinct spans | scalar | `14` | **`13`** | under NFKC, four tokens share one span |

`n_chars != n_chars'` is the whole part in one row. Every downstream assumption that the tokenizer sees
what you passed it is false the moment that row differs.

---

## When it breaks

**The failure is that nothing raises.** There is no `NormalizationWarning`. The measured output above is
what a correctly-installed, correctly-configured library does. It is only a bug relative to an expectation
you were never told you had.

**The corpus-scale version.** Run a corpus through NFKC and every ligature, fraction, superscript, circled
number, full-width Latin character and non-breaking space is rewritten. On typeset-derived text — anything
extracted from PDFs — ligatures are everywhere. Your model then cannot produce `ﬁ`, because it has never
seen one, and cannot distinguish `x²` from `x2`. Sometimes that is what you want. It should be a decision.

**The train/serve version, which is the dangerous one.** Train with NFKC, serve without, and:

```text
training saw : 'The file costs 1⁄2 a dollar.'
serving sends: 'The ﬁle costs ½ a dollar.'
```

Different ids, no error, and on ASCII-only test text the two paths agree perfectly. **Silent Failure #2,
hiding behind Silent Failure #5.** Your test suite passes because your tests are in English with no
typography.

**The `Ġ1` row is a specific trap.** It claims span `(13, 15)`, which is `' ½'` — so a highlighter drawing
that span paints the space *and* the fraction, then paints the fraction again four more times. Overlapping
highlights that darken on redraw are the visible symptom, and they get reported as a rendering bug.

---

## In production

**Canonical normalization is nearly always right; compatibility normalization is a decision.** NFC costs
almost nothing and fixes a real problem — the same text encoded two ways, from Day 10 part
[1.5](../../../day-010-unicode-code-points-bytes/parts/01-code-points-and-graphemes/1.5-the-string-that-was-not-equal-to-itself.md).
NFKC additionally throws away distinctions, and whether that is acceptable depends entirely on the task.
For search it usually helps. For code, mathematics or anything typographic it is destructive.

**Modern byte-level tokenizers mostly use no normalizer at all**, which is why part
[2.1](2.1-five-stages-not-one.md)'s pipeline showed `normalizer: None` and why that is a real
configuration rather than an omission. Byte-level BPE already represents everything, so normalization buys
little and costs the round trip.

**When a normalizer is used, it ships in the tokenizer file.** That is the fix for the train/serve
mismatch, and part [3.1](../03-the-remaining-gap/3.1-the-file-is-the-contract.md) is where you see the
`normalizer` key sitting in the JSON. A serving stack that constructs its normalizer in code instead of
loading it from the file has reintroduced exactly the bug the file exists to prevent.

**Offset-dependent products document this limitation.** Systems that highlight source spans either forbid
destructive normalizers outright, or keep the original string alongside the normalized one and accept that
some spans are approximate. There is no third option, because the information is genuinely gone.

## Check yourself

```python
from tokenizers import normalizers

s = "The ﬁle costs ½ a dollar."
plain, nfkc = build(norm=None), build(norm=normalizers.NFKC())

# 1. the normalizer changes the length - print it, do not assume it
n = nfkc.normalizer.normalize_str(s)
print(f"{len(s)} chars in -> {len(n)} chars after NFKC")
assert len(n) != len(s), "your probe has no compatibility characters - pick a better one"

# 2. the eval that can go red, in both directions
assert plain.decode(plain.encode(s).ids) == s, "no-normalizer round trip should hold"
assert nfkc.decode(nfkc.encode(s).ids) != s, "NFKC round trip should NOT hold"
print("round trip:", repr(nfkc.decode(nfkc.encode(s).ids)))

# 3. which offset invariants survive?
def report(tok, label):
    o = tok.encode(s)
    tiling = all(b1 == a2 for (_, b1), (a2, _) in zip(o.offsets, o.offsets[1:]))
    print(f"{label:14s} start0={o.offsets[0][0] == 0}  "
          f"end={o.offsets[-1][1] == len(s)}  tiles={tiling}  "
          f"distinct spans={len(set(o.offsets))}/{len(o.offsets)}")

report(plain, "no normalizer")
report(nfkc, "NFKC")

# 4. the specific collapse: some span is claimed by more than one token
from collections import Counter
spans = Counter(nfkc.encode(s).offsets)
worst, n_tokens = spans.most_common(1)[0]
print(f"span {worst} is claimed by {n_tokens} tokens -> {s[worst[0]:worst[1]]!r}")
assert n_tokens > 1, "no collapsed span - your probe did not trigger the failure"
```

**Line by line:**

- Assertion 2 is the eval that can go red, and the second half is the unusual one: it asserts the round trip
  **fails**. A test that only checked the passing case would give you no warning if a future library version
  changed this behaviour. Pinning the failure pins the boundary.
- `assert len(n) != len(s)` guards the probe itself. If you swap in your own test string and it contains no
  compatibility characters, every later assertion would pass vacuously — this line makes that loud. Tests
  that silently test nothing are worse than no tests.
- `Counter(offsets).most_common(1)` finds the most-claimed span directly, rather than looping to look for
  duplicates. It reports both *which* span collapsed and *how badly*, which is what you would want in a
  real bug report.
- `report` prints four properties rather than asserting them, because the interesting result is the
  *pattern* — three survive, one does not — and a bare assertion would hide that.
