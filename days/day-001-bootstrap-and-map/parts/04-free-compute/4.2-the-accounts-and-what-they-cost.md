# 4.2 — The accounts: what free compute actually costs you

## One-line answer

Free GPU sessions are not free of cost — they are free of **money**, and paid for in revocability,
opacity and data exposure — so the accounts are set up today with those three costs named, priced
and designed around rather than discovered on Day 67.

---

## The story

Somebody is given access to a shared resource at no charge. It is genuinely generous and it genuinely
works.

They start treating it as though it were theirs. Work accumulates in it. A long task is started with
the assumption it will finish. Files are left in the expectation they will still be there tomorrow.

Then it is reclaimed — not maliciously, not as a punishment, simply because that is what shared
resources do when somebody else needs them or a limit is reached. The work in progress is gone. The
files are gone. Nothing was promised and nothing was broken.

The loss was avoidable and nobody was careless. **What was missing was a clear statement, in advance,
of what the arrangement actually guaranteed** — which was nothing — so every design decision was made
against an assumption that had never been checked.

---

## The idea in plain language

"Free" means no invoice. It does not mean no cost. Free compute is paid for in three currencies, and
each one changes how you must write code:

**Revocability.** The session can end at any moment: a usage limit, a busy period, an idle timeout, a
closed laptop lid. Nothing is owed to you. The design consequence is absolute — **anything that
cannot survive being killed at a random instant is a bug**, not bad luck. Checkpoint-and-resume is
not an optimisation here, it is a correctness requirement.

**Opacity.** You do not choose the hardware, and it varies between sessions. The GPU you get today
may not be the one you get tomorrow, and your numbers change with it. The design consequence is that
**hardware goes in the `RUNS.md` row** (part
[3.2](../03-the-ledgers/3.2-runs-md-turns-anecdote-into-result.md)) — otherwise two runs are not
comparable and you will not know why.

**Data exposure.** The machine is not yours. Anything you upload is on somebody else's
infrastructure, and anything the notebook prints may be stored in the file (part
[2.3](../02-secrets-and-tokens/2.3-the-token-that-leaked.md)). The design consequence is that this
project uses **synthetic or openly-licensed data only**, and the token is read-scope.

The fourth cost is subtler and worth naming because it distorts judgement: **free compute is metered
in a currency you cannot buy more of.** When a session is burning and something is not working, the
temptation is to skip the careful step — not to record the row, not to run the second seed, not to
verify the config. That is exactly backwards, and it is why the plan denominates budgets in
**GPU-minutes and session count** rather than dollars: naming the currency is what makes it feel
spendable rather than infinite.

---

## Why Akshara needs it

Five days in this plan are T1, and each is a single session's worth of work that cannot be casually
repeated: **67** (pretraining), **89** (LoRA fine-tune), **94** (DPO), **123** (the vision–language
projector), **134** (diffusion).

Setting the accounts up today, rather than on the morning of Day 67, buys three things:

- **The token exists and has the right scope**, so the first thing you do in a metered session is not
  a browser detour to create one (part [2.1](../02-secrets-and-tokens/2.1-the-token-in-one-place.md)).
- **You have seen what hardware you are actually offered**, which is an input to the sizing arithmetic
  on Day 66. Sizing a model against a GPU you have never checked is guessing.
- **You have measured the session's real limits** — how long, how much disk, how much RAM — which are
  the numbers Day 66 needs and which are not reliably documented anywhere.

That last point is Principle 8 in its most practical form. The free tiers of these platforms change,
and any number written here from memory would be wrong by the time you read it. **You measure them,
today, and write them in `docs/PACKAGES.md` with a date.**

## The mechanism

Three accounts, all free, none requiring payment details.

| Account | Why | Scope to request |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Hugging Face** | datasets and model weights, from Day 61 | **read** token only (Principle 13) |
| **Google Colab** | T1 notebook GPU | free tier; no subscription |
| **Kaggle** | second T1 option; different quota, often longer sessions | free tier; phone verification unlocks the GPU |

Two, not one, deliberately: quotas are per-platform, and a day that fails on one can run on the
other. Redundancy in a resource you cannot buy is worth the ten minutes of signing up.

Now **measure** what you were actually given — do not read it from a marketing page:

```python
# Paste into a notebook cell on the free GPU platform, with the GPU runtime selected.
import subprocess, shutil, psutil, platform, datetime
print("date        :", datetime.date.today().isoformat())
print("platform    :", platform.platform())
print("cpu count   :", psutil.cpu_count())
print("ram GB      :", round(psutil.virtual_memory().total / 1e9, 1))
print("disk free GB:", round(shutil.disk_usage('/').free / 1e9, 1))
print(subprocess.run(["nvidia-smi",
                      "--query-gpu=name,memory.total,driver_version",
                      "--format=csv,noheader"],
                     capture_output=True, text=True).stdout.strip())
```

**Line by line:**
- `datetime.date.today()` is first because **the row is worthless without a date** — free tiers move,
  and a measurement with no date is a claim about "now" that silently becomes a claim about "always"
  (part [3.1](../03-the-ledgers/3.1-what-a-ledger-is-for.md)).
- `psutil.virtual_memory().total` is host RAM, which is a separate constraint from GPU memory and the
  one that bites when tokenizing a corpus on Day 64. Both numbers matter and people only remember the
  GPU one.
- `shutil.disk_usage('/')` is the session's scratch space. Day 64 writes a tokenized corpus and Day
  67 writes checkpoints; if this number is smaller than you assumed, you find out now rather than at
  step 8000.
- `nvidia-smi --query-gpu=...` asks the driver directly. `memory.total` is the number Day 66's sizing
  arithmetic consumes, and `name` is what goes in the `Hardware` column of every `RUNS.md` row.
- `capture_output=True, text=True` returns the output as a string rather than printing it to a stream
  the notebook might not capture — which matters because you are going to copy this into a ledger.

Record what it printed, with the date:

```text
| Package | Version | Date | Day | Why |
| ------- | ------- | ---- | --- | --- |
| colab-gpu (observed) | Tesla T4, 15360 MiB, driver 550.x | 2026-08-26 | 1 | The T1 device. Measured with `nvidia-smi`, not read from a docs page. Day 66 sizes Akshara against this number. |
| colab-host (observed) | 12.7 GB RAM, 78 GB disk free | 2026-08-26 | 1 | Host limits — the constraint on Day 64's corpus tokenization, which is CPU and disk bound. |
```

**Line by line:**
- The rows go in `docs/PACKAGES.md` because they are versioned facts about the environment, observed
  on a date — exactly what that ledger is for.
- `(observed)` in the name makes it unmistakable that this is a measurement rather than a
  specification. The distinction is Day 0's part
  [1.3](../../../day-000-toolchain-skeleton-driver/parts/01-the-toolchain/1.3-observed-is-not-available.md):
  offered versus observed.
- **Do not copy the numbers above.** They are illustrative. Yours will differ and yours are the ones
  Day 66 will use.

Getting the token onto the machine — and note what is *not* happening:

```python
# In the notebook, use the platform's own secret mechanism. Never paste the token into a cell.
from google.colab import userdata
import os
os.environ["HF_TOKEN"] = userdata.get("HF_TOKEN")
print("token present:", bool(os.environ.get("HF_TOKEN")))
```

**Line by line:**
- `userdata.get` reads from the platform's secret store, which is not part of the notebook file. The
  token never appears in the JSON that gets committed.
- Setting `os.environ` means the rest of your code — which reads `os.environ["HF_TOKEN"]` per part
  [2.2](../02-secrets-and-tokens/2.2-reading-env-without-a-dependency.md) — works unchanged. **This
  is why `load_env` lets the real environment win:** no branch is needed for the notebook case.
- `print("token present:", bool(...))` prints a **boolean, never the value**. Printing even a prefix
  writes it into the notebook's outputs, which is precisely the leak from part
  [2.3](../02-secrets-and-tokens/2.3-the-token-that-leaked.md).

## When it breaks

The loud failure that ends a session without warning:

```console
^C
Your session crashed after using all available RAM.
```

Host RAM, not GPU memory — the mistake almost everybody makes first, because "out of memory" on a GPU
machine is assumed to mean the GPU. It usually happens while loading a dataset rather than while
training. The number that would have predicted it is the one you measured above.

The other loud one, at the quota boundary:

```console
You cannot currently connect to a GPU due to usage limits in Colab.
```

No appeal and no timeline. This is revocability arriving as a refusal rather than an interruption,
and it is why there are two platforms.

**The silent version, which is the expensive one.** The session is reclaimed mid-run. The notebook
shows a disconnected kernel — no traceback, no error, just a stopped cell. If nothing was
checkpointed, the GPU-minutes are simply gone, and there is no artifact recording that they were
spent. Worse: without a `RUNS.md` row written *at launch*, there is no record the run happened at
all, so the next attempt cannot learn from it.

Detect it — and survive it — by writing the row **before** the run rather than after:

```bash
git hash-object configs/base-12m.yaml | cut -c1-7
```

**Line by line:**
- Compute the config hash at launch and write the `RUNS.md` row immediately, with `Outcome` left as
  `running`. If the session dies, the row is already there and you amend it to `preempted`.
- A ledger row written **after** a successful run only ever records successes, which is the failure
  part [3.2](../03-the-ledgers/3.2-runs-md-turns-anecdote-into-result.md) warns about — and the
  runs that teach you most are the ones that did not finish.
- Day 58 (checkpoint and resume) is the mechanism that makes a pre-empted run recoverable rather than
  wasted, and it is taught **before** Day 67 for exactly this reason.

## In production

**What a research engineer does instead of the teaching version.** They treat every machine as
disposable, including the ones they pay for. Spot and pre-emptible instances are the paid equivalent
of a free session — cheaper precisely because they can be reclaimed — and the engineering response is
identical: checkpoint frequently, make resume the default path rather than an exception, and record
the hardware in the run's metadata. **The discipline this curriculum forces on you because you have
no money is the same discipline that saves large teams the most money**, which is Principle 15
stated as a fact rather than a consolation.

**What degrades at scale.** Session-based work does not compose. One notebook is fine; twenty
experiments across two platforms with different quotas is unmanageable by hand, and the answer is a
queue and a scheduler. What survives from here is the metadata discipline: every run identified by a
config hash, a seed and a hardware string, so results remain comparable across machines you did not
choose.

**The failure that only shows with real data.** Hardware heterogeneity. The free tier gives you
whatever is available, and a run that fits comfortably one day fails to fit the next. Numbers move
too — throughput, and sometimes the loss, because reduced-precision behaviour differs between
devices. **This is why the `Hardware` column is not bureaucratic**: it is the only thing that will
let you explain a discrepancy you would otherwise attribute to your change.

**The review comment.** *"This reports a training time — on what hardware, and was it the same device
as the baseline?"* On free compute the answer is frequently no, and that alone can explain a
difference somebody was about to draw a conclusion from.

**The interview question.** *"How do you run experiments on pre-emptible hardware?"* The answer that
shows experience: checkpoint on a step interval rather than an epoch, make resume idempotent, write
run metadata at launch rather than at exit, and treat interruption as the expected case. What is
being probed is whether you have ever lost a long run — everyone has, once.

**Compute tier: T0 to set up, T1 to measure.** Creating the accounts costs nothing. The measurement
cell spends a few GPU-minutes of a session and produces numbers Day 66 cannot proceed without —
which makes it the cheapest T1 spend in the entire plan.
